She got in at twenty past eight with two things to tell and told neither.

It was not a decision. She opened the door, set the bag down on the hall floor,
and noticed — with the bag already on the floor — that she had not put anything
in order on the way. Not the empty house, not Block C. She had spent the whole
ride on the 12 looking out the window.

Eleven years doing that small job, and that Thursday she simply did not do it.

---

The door of the back room was pulled to.

She could hear his voice with the pauses in it, without the words, the way it
always was.

Rita stood a few seconds in the hall with her hand still on the strap, and what
she thought was not about him: it was that if she knocked on the door now, he
would open it, and sit down, and listen to both things all the way through, and
he would be good at it.

Then she went to the kitchen.

---

She sat down.

This has to be said this way, because it is small and it is not. Rita sits at
home every day: she sits to eat, she sits to fold, she sits on the edge of the
bed to take off her sneakers. She sits doing. There is nothing in her life that
is sitting.

That night she pulled out the chair, sat down, put both hands on the table, and
stayed.

The counter was clean. The pan from the day before was no longer there. There
was nothing to be done in that kitchen and she checked, the way you check,
knowing there was not.

---

Her earpiece is the size of an avocado pit, flat on one side, and it goes in
without pressing.

When it settles, it warms a little — not enough to call warm — and the kitchen
drops away behind it. It does not go silent: it goes halfway, as if someone had
laid a hand over the world. The refrigerator is still there, behind a wall.

She put in the earpiece and said the name out loud.

"Better. Regina Spektor."

The title and the singer, both, in the empty kitchen, to nobody, the way you
say a name you know whole.

---

It played.

There was no search, no list, none of that instant in which a thing loads. She
finished saying the name and the piano was already coming in — the piano comes
in on its own and in a hurry, in this song, and always has — and it came in at
the volume she wanted, which is a thing nobody has ever asked her.

Rita knows this song all the way through. She knows where the voice breaks in
the middle of a word, on purpose, like someone stumbling without stumbling, and
she knows it comes back twice after that.

It is a happy song. The piano is choppy and never rests for a moment, there is
a light kick drum underneath the whole time, and you can walk fast to it.

The words aren't.

She understands the words. She understands the words of everything, in any
language, and has never noticed that she does.

Where she got this song, she does not know. There is no car, no glove
compartment, no person who put it on.

Not once has she found that strange.

---

A delivery truck went by on the street below, one of the long ones that come
after eight, and the noise came up through the window.

The volume of the music rose with it, exactly as much, and came down again when
the truck turned the corner.

Rita did not notice that. She noticed the truck.

---

Elias appeared in the kitchen doorway with his glass in his hand, halfway
through the song.

She took out the earpiece.

"You okay?" he said.

"Fine."

He filled the glass. He looked at her sitting at the table with nothing in
front of her, both hands on the table, and smiled at her the way he smiles,
which is a kind smile and is not a small thing.

"Nice to see you sitting still," he said.

"Yeah."

"Tomorrow I'll do that thing with the pan."

"Okay."

He went off with the glass, and in the hall the door was pulled to again, and
the voice came back with its pauses.

He did not ask about her day, and Rita did not notice that, that night. She
would notice on Friday, on the 12, putting three things in ascending order.

---

A third of the song was left when her hand went up and took out the earpiece.

She did not decide. The hand went first, and she sat with the earpiece between
her fingers looking at it, and she had no reason ready to give anyone.

She kept the earpiece in her hand. She did not put it back.

---

The kitchen came back.

The refrigerator, the neighbor's pipe, someone dragging a chair upstairs. It was
not silence — her home is never silence, and she knows every one of those noises
by heart and what each one means.

She stayed there with both hands on the table and the earpiece between her
fingers, and the song that had been left half-finished did not keep playing in
her head, because that does not happen to her.

After a while she understood what the loudest noise in the kitchen was.

It was his voice in the other room. With the pauses.
