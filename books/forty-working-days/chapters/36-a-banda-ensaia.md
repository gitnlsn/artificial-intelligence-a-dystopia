There are thirty-one of them and they cannot start together.

That has been the problem since the first rehearsal and it was still the problem
at ten past five on Friday: Dória raises her arm, holds it, and brings it down —
and out comes a boom of bass drum, and a piece of trumpet a little behind, and
the snare coming in when it was already late, and someone way at the back who
never got to play at all.

And Dória stops everything with a single clap and says *again*.

---

That Friday it was seven times.

Nina counted. She sits on the two-step bleachers, next to the drinking fountain,
and counts the times, and the record is eleven, which was on a Wednesday, last
month.

On the fourth time it almost came out.

It almost came out and everybody felt it, and there was that little noise a band
makes when it knows it almost worked, half laugh and half snort, and Dória said
*there*, and said *again*, and the fifth time came out worse than all the ones
before.

---

Bel plays snare.

The snares are in the second row, on the left side, and there are three snares,
and Bel is the middle one.

Bel is good. Nina knows Bel is good because you can hear it when she stops —
there was a day, about two months ago, when she was sick and didn't come, and
that day the whole band fell apart, and Nina spent the whole afternoon secretly
very pleased.

She never told Bel that.

---

Dória is a pain and Nina hates Dória and Dória is right.

This is something Nina would admit under torture and not any other way. Dória
picks on Bel. Dória suspended Bel for two days for something half the world does.
Dória has a favorite and the favorite is the glockenspiel girl, who is bad.

And even so, when Dória stops everything and says *again*, it's because it was
wrong.

It was always wrong. Nina, who doesn't play anything and can't read music, can
already hear when it's wrong, because she has spent six weeks on those
bleachers.

---

At twenty to six, on the seventh, Dória changed one thing.

She stopped counting out loud and told everybody to look at the bass drum.

Only at the bass drum. Not at her, not at the music, not at the person next to
them.

"You don't come in on my arm," she said. "You come in on the bass drum. If the
bass drum is wrong, you come in wrong together. That's better."

---

It was the weirdest thing Nina heard that week.

*If it's wrong, come in wrong together.*

She kept thinking about it and didn't think it was right. If it's wrong, it has
to be right. You fix it, you do it again, you do it until it comes out.

But the band came in together on the eighth time.

It came in together and it was still wrong — Nina heard it, there was something
out of tune in the middle, and one of the trombones was late the whole time — and
even so, for the first time in six weeks, thirty-one people started at the same
moment.

And it was good.

Not good like being right. Good some other way, which Nina can't name and which
made her straighten her back on the bleachers without noticing she had
straightened it.

---

Dória didn't praise them. She said *that's it, Monday without fail*, and then
remembered there wasn't going to be a Monday because the parade was on Saturday,
and was visibly annoyed about it.

Then she said:

"Tomorrow at eight fifteen at school. Anybody late doesn't march."

---

Bel came over to the bleachers putting her sticks in her back pocket.

"Did you see the eighth?"

"I saw."

"We came in together."

"You came in together wrong."

"Together," said Bel.

---

Nina walked home with her mother, who came to get her.

And it was on the way, passing the lot with the dog, that she decided something
she didn't have the slightest trouble deciding, because it had already been
decided for about fifteen minutes:

She wasn't going to see robots.

Everybody at school talked about the robots, and Lu had made up that one was the
size of a horse, and Lu's brother had seen it, and Nina thought that was
interesting the way you think something is interesting.

She was going because of the eighth.
