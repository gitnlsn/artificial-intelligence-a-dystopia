The pencil was a 2B and he sharpened it with a razor blade, never with a
sharpener, because a sharpener eats too much lead. He had learned that in a
newsroom where nobody had money to waste on lead, and he went on doing it that
way sixty years later, sitting in the armchair with a piece of newspaper on his
lap to catch the shavings — newspaper he kept for that, because no newspaper
came anymore and what there was in the house was what there already was.

The blade came down slowly. He turned the pencil against his thumb, took off
three, four thin slices, blew. The point came out long and a little crooked, as
it always had.

The book lay open on his left leg, face down, waiting.

It was a 1990 copy, paperback, the white spine creased, bought at a bookshop on
Aldan Street that was now an agency of some kind. On the title page the price
was still there in pencil, in a money that no longer existed, written by the
hand of someone who probably no longer did either. Aurel liked that number. Not
for the amount, which was untranslatable, but because someone had written it
there by hand, and the hand had rested its wrist on the page, and you could see
the mark of the wrist on the page if the light fell from the side.

He set the book right and found the line where he had stopped.

*The first thing to observe about the world of the 1780s is that it was at once
much smaller and much larger than ours.*

He read it again. Not because it was difficult. He read it again because it was
firm.

A man had sat down in a chair, in some year around 1960, and had written that
about a decade a hundred and eighty years before him, and had not hesitated.
*At once much smaller and much larger.* Not *perhaps it was*. Not *it could be
argued that*. It was. The man knew the size of the world of 1780 better than
anyone alive in 1780 had known it, and he knew it because he was far away. The
distance was not a problem he had got around. The distance was the instrument.
It was what you measured with.

Aurel let the pencil rest in the fold of the book.

The paragraph went on. It was smaller in the geographical sense, it said,
because even the most educated and best-informed people alive at the time — and
here the author paused to give a name, Alexander von Humboldt, scientist and
traveler, with the dates in parentheses like someone making an introduction —
knew only patches of the inhabited globe.

Patches. Aurel liked the word.

Then came the list, and the list was the best thing in the book, and he had
never managed to keep the list. Two or three things stayed, loose and out of
order — the floor of the sea, of which nothing was known; mountains measured
precisely on one continent and badly guessed on another — and the map stayed,
which was what mattered: blank spaces crossed by the trails of people who
traded.

Blank spaces. Not unknown: blank. There was paper there, and the paper was
empty, and everyone who looked at that map saw the same blank in the same place
and knew that it was what was not yet known.

And then — and it was for this sentence that Aurel had bought the book, if a
person buys a book for a sentence — the author came down the scale, to the
illiterate Sicilian peasant who spent his whole life inside a handful of
kilometers and for whom everything beyond them would be unknown forever.

Aurel had read that part three times over thirty years and always stopped in
the same place.

Because the Sicilian peasant had a small world, and it is easy to pity him for
it, and pity is the lazy way to read the sentence. What that man had, and it
was enormous, was the **edge**. He knew how far it went. He knew the names of
the three villages, knew the distance between them in hours of walking, knew
that past that hill there was something he knew nothing about, and knew, which
is different, that he did not know. His world was eight kilometers across and
it had an outline. It fit whole inside one person's head, and the person could
say where it ended.

Aurel was eighty-two, had read four thousand books and revised thirty-seven
thousand pages, and could not say where the thing he was living inside of
ended.

Neither world could be taken in whole, that was the joke. Only one could not be
taken in because it was too big and the other because it had no rim at all, and
the Sicilian at least knew the difference between what he knew and the rest.
There was a hill. On the other side of the hill was something else. Aurel had
no hill. Aurel had a living room with two windows and a quantity of world coming
in through them that never grew smaller and never arranged itself anywhere, and
none of it had a blank on the map saying *here we do not know*.

And that was not even the worst. The worst was that he looked out the window
and saw Aldan Street, and Aldan Street was written.

The price of coffee hung about two hand-spans above the awning, in thin clear
lettering, the number larger than the rest, and it moved with the awning if the
wind moved it. The timetable for the 41 sat above the stop, three lines, and
the top one kept changing its minute. The name of whatever now occupies the
place where the bookshop was lay on the front of the building as if it were
painted there, and it was not, and Aurel knows it is not because the front
underneath is brick and he saw the brick in 1994.

The letters do not glow. They take the tone of the wall, a little lighter, and
draw back when he looks at something else. When a car passes in front of a
price, the price stays behind the car, the way it should. Everything the right
size, everything out of the way. It worked very well. It had never failed.

What he no longer knew, and had no way of knowing, is what was written on Aldan
Street for the gentleman in 408.

It was not that he suspected he was being deceived. He probably was not. It was
that the street had stopped being a place where two people read the same thing
and had become a place where two people read, and nobody noticed the
difference because the difference does not show: the two of them look at the
same lamppost and the two of them answer quickly and the two of them are right.

Outside, the afternoon was at the point where the light turns the color of old
paper and the things inside a house become sharper than they have been all day.
The living room had two windows and he kept both of them open until late, even
when it turned cold, even with the noise of Aldan Street coming up. He liked the
noise. It was a noise of buses, of gates, of people getting off with shopping
bags. The 41 came every eleven minutes and he knew the sound of the 41.

It took him two winters to understand that the house was paying for it. He left
both windows open on nights when nobody would, and the room stayed at the same
temperature as the day before, without a draft, without a sound, without
anything coming on anywhere.
Nobody ever told him what it cost. There was nowhere to look and he never went
looking.

He got up. He did that now, got up in the middle of reading, and it was not
restlessness: it was the leg. If he sat still for more than twenty minutes the
left leg began to tingle from the knee down, and the thing to do was walk to the
bookcase and back.

He walked to the bookcase and back.

Down below, the 41 pulled in. It has had no driver for some twenty years and it
stops in the same place as always, and what changed was not the timetable — the
timetable got better — it was the sound. There used to be an engine that rose
before the bus pulled away and now there is nothing, and what you hear from his
window is only the door and the people. Aurel learned the new sound the same
way he had learned the old one, without meaning to, in about two years, and
sometimes he still waited for the engine.

The apartment was too big and he knew it the same way he knew the sound of the
41: without thinking, and all the time. Three bedrooms. Two of them closed for
eleven years, not out of any drama, only because cleaning an empty room is
something a person stops doing. Ilse had chosen the apartment in 2011 for the
brightness of the living room and because the kitchen had a window, and she
said a kitchen without a window is a closet you cook in.

The kitchen had a window. He looked at it almost every day with the late
satisfaction of someone who was persuaded and took a long time to admit it.

He went to the bookcase again. He was not looking for anything. He ran his
finger along the spines of the four volumes, which stood in the order in which
they had been written and not the order in which he had bought them, which was
already an opinion. *Revolution. Capital. Empire. Extremes.* Four books and four
names.

Someone had had to give them those names.

That was what he had been circling since the start of the afternoon without
being able to reach it, and now he reached it sideways, looking at four spines.
Someone had sat down and decided that that, that block of years, was called the
age of revolution. That the next block was capital's. That after it came
empire, and after that the extremes. They were not beautiful names. They were
working names, names for being able to talk about a thing without having to
describe the whole of it every time. But to give a name like that you have to be
outside the block. The block has to be over, or at least you have to be able to
pretend, honestly, that it is.

Aurel went back to the armchair and did not sit. He stood behind it with both
hands on its back, the way he used to stand behind Bendt's chair when the two of
them were arguing over a headline and he already knew he was going to win.

"And this one," he said, to the room. "This one is the age of what."

His voice came out louder than he had meant it to. Talking to himself did not
bother him. It had started after Ilse, and he had gone through a phase of
thinking it a symptom, and then he had given up thinking so. Talking out loud
was a tool. A sentence that does not stand up out loud does not stand up.

He tried.

The age of machines, no. *Machine* is a word from 1800. A machine is a loom, a
boiler, a thing with metal parts you take apart on a workbench. It would not do
for what was happening, and worse: naming by the machine was the mistake he had
spent forty years watching other people make at the close of an edition, when a
headline was missing and someone suggested the name of the device. The device
is never the story.

The information age, then. But they had already called it that, had called it
that when he was young, and the name had aged badly and ended up looking like a
brochure.

The age of convenience. That came close. He stood still on that one for a few
seconds, his hands on the back of the armchair, because there was something
there. Only *convenience* is a small word, an advertising word, and he could
smell an advertising word three blocks away. A whole period is not named with a
word that would fit on a poster.

The age of...

And then nothing came.

Nothing came in the literal sense: he was standing, his mouth ready, and the
sentence did not go on. It was not a lapse of memory. He knew very well what a
lapse of memory was, he had them already, and they came with a small fright and
a quick rearranging inside. This was something else. The word was not missing.
**There was no word missing.** There was no place from which to look and see the
whole block and name it, because for that there has to be an outside, and the
outside was made of other people looking at the same thing at the same time and
disagreeing about it out loud, and that he could no longer find anywhere in the
city.

He stood a little longer. The leg was fine. He had no excuse at all for
standing.

When he joined the newsroom, in 1988, the reviser was the last to see the page
and the first to take the blame. He wrote nothing. That was the beauty of it.
The reviser had no publishable opinion and was nevertheless the person who made
sure the page corresponded to something: that a name was spelled the way its
owner spelled it, that the number of the law was the number of the law, that the
city was in the country it was in, that the headline did not promise what the
story did not deliver. It was a job of correspondence. He sometimes explained it
that way, to people who asked, and almost nobody asked.

And the thing it took him thirty years to understand, and that he understood
only after he retired, is that the correspondence was not with the truth. It
was more modest and much harder. It was with the neighbor.

Because what he did, along with four thousand people in four thousand
newsrooms, was keep something standing that nobody had agreed to keep standing:
a page that was the same page in his house and in his neighbor's.

He had tried to explain this to Mira once and had chosen his words badly. He
said that nobody reads the same page today, and she answered that she did, that
they had talked about the same thing on Tuesday, and she was right and he was
right and the two of them were talking about different things. It is not
censorship and nobody is missing anything. It is that what reaches a person is
put together for that person, and it is good, and it is good exactly to
measure — and two things made good to measure for two different people are not
the same thing, and there is no place in the world where the two can be laid
side by side and checked.

They could disagree with everything written there. They did disagree, as it
happens, and with pleasure, and Bendt and he had nearly come to blows in 1996
over an editorial. But they disagreed *about the same thing*. The fight had an
object. The object had ink, had a page number, had mistakes you could point to
with a finger and demand an erratum for in the next edition.

Erratum. He had not thought of that word in years and it arrived whole, in the
type size it had, always smaller, always at the foot of the page.

An erratum is an extraordinary object and nobody ever paid it any mind. It is a
piece of paper that says: here is what we said, here is what it was, and the
difference between the two is ours and we own it. For an erratum to exist there
has to be a *we*, a *yesterday*, and a reader who can demand one. It needs
three things at the same time.

He could not have said when the three had stopped existing together. There was
no day. It was like the leg: at some point you notice it has been a while.

It was almost dark. He noticed because the text on his left leg had gone gray,
and not because the room had grown darker, since the room had not grown darker —
the house had been raising the light since a little after six, slowly, the way
the house did things, without warning and without mistakes. At that time of
year it began at about twenty past six and went up to a point he had never
needed to choose.

It was comfortable. That was the honest word and he had no particular wish to
say it.

Aurel went to the lamp in the corner and switched it on by hand, the little
screw knob, which gave a click. The house understood the click and drew back,
and the light in the room came down half a tone so as not to fight with his. He
stood watching it happen. It was not stubbornness — or it was, but a
stubbornness with an address. It was the click. He wanted the light in the room
to be something someone had decided, and not something that kept arriving.

He sat. The seat of the armchair had been too deep on the right side for years.

And it was then, with the book open on his lap again and the lamplight falling
from the side, that he saw it.

It was in the third line of the next paragraph, at the turn of the page. The
word *the* at the end of one line and the word *the* at the start of the next.
Repeated. One *the* too many, planted there in 1990, in the middle of a sentence
about the extent of the oceans, and nobody had seen it.

He laughed — a short sound, through the nose.

It was the most ordinary mistake there is, and the most impossible to catch.
The eye does not read twice across the turn of a line; the eye trusts. It was
the first mistake they taught you to look for and the one that most often got
through. He had caught that mistake some three hundred times in his life and
had let heaven knows how many go by.

This one was fifty-seven years old. It had got past the publisher's reviser,
the printer, three reprintings at least, and the whole life of the book's
owner, who might be him or might be the person of the wrist on the title page.

And it was there for anyone. That was the part he took too long to say without
sounding sentimental, and so he did not say it: if someone else opened that
copy at that page, they would see that extra *the*. The same one. Not a version
of the *the*. The object was stubborn that way. Ink on paper does not adjust
itself to whoever is looking. Nobody was going to publish an erratum for a book
from 1990. The translator was dead. Hobsbawm was dead. Aldan Street had become
an agency of some kind.

He picked up the pencil.

He brought the point to the margin, and his hand made on its own the stroke the
hand knew: the *deleatur*, the struck d, that old mark that means *take this
out*, which he had learned from a man called Márcio who was already old in 1988
and who said the mark came from the copyists and that he, Aurel, owed it
respect.

He marked it. He closed the stroke. He checked that it was legible in the
margin, the way you check.

Then he sat for a while with the pencil in his hand, without turning the page,
in the middle of a room with the right light, listening to the 41 arrive down
below eleven minutes after the other one, without knowing what era he was in,
but having, there, one thing corrected.
