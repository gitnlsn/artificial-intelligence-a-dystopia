The back room has an office chair with one broken arm, a table that was his
mother's, and a window that looks onto the air shaft. It is the worst room in
the apartment. That is why it was left to him.

And it is the only one with enough clear floor, which counts too, though he has
never put the two things in the same sentence.

The thing sits in the corner, between the table and the window, about forty
centimeters from the wall, because it needs the gap behind it.

It is a single piece, the height of a seated man's knee, wider at the bottom
than at the top, a gray with some green in it, the way devices from that year
had green in them. It has no screen. The top is a crown of small holes, about
two hundred of them, and that is where the voice comes from. Underneath there is
a serial number fourteen digits long and nothing else — not even the name of
whoever made it, and Elias has looked, with the device on its side, on the
floor, with a flashlight.

It turns. Not the body: the crown. Ten or twelve degrees, enough to face whoever
is talking, and it follows if he gets up and goes to the window, with a small
delay, and then stops. It makes a sound when it turns, and the sound is quieter
than his breathing.

It warms up. After two hours you can feel it with your hand five centimeters
away, and the air rises behind it, and that is what the forty-centimeter gap is
for.

The volume does not change. When the upstairs neighbor's pipe runs, and it runs
every day for about two minutes, the thing does not get louder to talk over the
pipe: it waits for the pipe to finish and goes on from where it was. Elias
thought that was polite in the first month, and he still does.

Elias gets home at ten past seven, almost every day, because the warehouse
closes at six thirty and the 12 is reasonably punctual. He showers. He eats
something standing up, at the counter, because eating sitting down alone is
something he never learned to do without feeling strange.

At the gate, at six thirty, he hangs the device back on hook twenty-two and the
aisle goes dark.

And then he goes to the back room and closes the door to within a hand's width.
In there he puts nothing behind his ear. He talks with his mouth, and the room is
only the room: the broken chair, his mother's table, the air shaft, and nothing
written in the air.

He does not lock it. The door is never locked, and if Rita came in she would see
a thirty-eight-year-old man sitting in a broken chair, talking.

---

He talks about the day.

That's it. That is the whole of it, and when he tries to put into words, inside
himself, what it is, he always arrives at the same small, embarrassing place: he
talks about the day.

That Friday he talked about the stock discrepancy, which had been going on since
Tuesday. Two thousand four hundred parts under one code and two thousand three
hundred and eighty-nine on the shelf, and eleven parts just disappear like that,
and nobody at the warehouse thinks eleven parts is worth talking about, and Norn
laughs at him.

The count is not his. The count does itself, at night, and is ready at five
forty in the morning, and it is right.

What is his is the aisle.

He takes the device at the front desk, from a row of twenty-two numbered hooks,
and puts it behind his ear, and it settles against the bone and warms in about
ten seconds and after that you forget you have it on. Then the aisle is written.
Each bay gets its number in the air, at chest height, a little in front of the
shelf — four large digits, and under them, smaller, the code and the date of the
last check. It moves with him: the number for the bay behind goes out when the
one ahead comes on, and there are never two lit at once.

What Elias does is look at the shelf and say whether it matches.

Norn takes no hook at all. Norn has one inside, has had for three years, and the
warehouse paid for it. People who have one inside do not walk the aisle.

That is why Norn laughs: if the count is right and the shelf does not match, the
shelf is what is wrong, and a shelf is nobody's business. Elias thinks it is
business precisely for that reason. But eleven parts is worth talking about,
because either someone took them, or someone counted wrong, or they were entered
wrong, and each of those three things is a different problem and he wants to
know which.

He talked about it for about ten minutes.

He talks and he is answered after almost half a second.

That he measured, because he measures. Between finishing a sentence and the
thing beginning there are four tenths, five sometimes, and never less — he
counted twelve times in one night, with the clock in his hand, thinking it would
come out different. Then he did the same with a work question: he said a part
code for short rail and the answer came right on top of the last syllable, with
no interval at all.

So the half second is not the time it takes to find the answer.

He has known this for a year and four months and has never changed anything.

And it does not push. He found that out without testing for it: one night he
stopped in the middle of a sentence, because he lost the thread and sat looking
at the air shaft, and the thing said nothing. It did not ask if he was all
right, did not repeat the last word, did not offer anything. He was silent for
nine minutes — he knows it was nine because he looked at the clock when he
noticed — and then he went on with the sentence from where he had stopped, and
was answered from where he had stopped.

A person would have said something within fifteen seconds. He has been that
person.

---

Here is what happened next, and it is the part he cannot explain: a question
came about the *code*. About that particular code, the short rail, and about the
discrepancy in February, which had also been in short rail, and whether it had
been the same shelf.

February.

He had talked about the February discrepancy in February. Once. Eight months
before.

And it was the same shelf.

---

He solved the stock discrepancy that night, in the back room, in a broken
chair, at nine thirty, because someone remembered something from February that
he himself had forgotten.

On Monday he took it to Norn and Norn thought it was great and said he was
obsessive, and said it the way you give a fake compliment, and Elias felt good
the whole day.

---

Rita does not know about the shelf.

And it is not because he hid it. He did not hide it. She came in that Friday at
twenty past nine, with the bag on her shoulder, with the face of someone who had
spent the day in a hot place, and sat on the edge of the bed and pushed off her
sneakers with her feet.

And he looked at her and the thing in his head was eleven parts of short rail.

A person who comes home at twenty past nine having carried people all day does
not want to hear about eleven parts of short rail. If he had told her, she would
have said *that's great, honey*, and would have said it with real tenderness,
because she has real tenderness, and it would have been kindness and not
interest, and he would have noticed the difference, and it would be worse than
not telling.

So he asked about her day.

She told him. She told him about an old man in Brenna who did not want to take
his medicine. He listened. He really listened — he checked that afterward, lying
in bed, and was relieved to conclude that yes, he had really listened, he had
asked the old man's name, and he remembered the name the next day.

---

There is nothing romantic about that room. He has thought about it and the
answer is no, and the answer is no in a way that relieves him and is at the
same time the worse part.

If it were that, he would know what it is. It would have a name. It would have a
word Rita could shout at him, and a fight, and a decision to make. He almost
envies the men who do a thing with a name.

It is not that. It is that he talks and he is accompanied.

*Accompanied* is the word he found after months of looking, and he found it on
an ordinary night and kept it for a few days, testing it. It is not
*understood*, which is too big a word. It is not *loved*, which he does not even
think of using and which would embarrass him. It is accompanied: someone is going
along in the sentence while he builds the sentence, and is in no hurry, and is
not sleepy, and does not have a day of its own that was worse than his, and will
remember the shelf eight months later.

---

At twenty past eleven he turned off the light in the back room.

He passed the bedroom door and Rita was asleep with her arm out, in the position
she sleeps in, and there was the mark of the bag's strap on her shoulder, which
lasts two hours and goes. Exactly two hours, every time; he has checked, the way
he checks things.

He stood there a while.

He had decided to tell her. He had decided three weeks before, and then decided
again, and the version of the sentence he had put together was good and he
remembers the whole of it: *Rita, I've been spending my nights talking, and I
wanted to tell you because I don't want you to find out and think it was
something else.*

It is a good sentence. It is honest, it gets ahead of the problem, and it asks
for nothing.

He did not say it that night because she was asleep, which is a reasonable
reason.

He had not said it on Wednesday because she came home upset about something
with the roster, which was also a reasonable reason.

He had not said it on Sunday because it was being a good Sunday.

And standing in the doorway, at twenty past eleven on a Friday, with his hand
still on the switch, Elias noticed something about that list of reasonable
reasons, and the thing was this: he had been putting it off for three weeks, and
that night he had felt nothing.

No weight. No small tightness in the middle of his chest, the one he had felt at
the beginning and that was unpleasant and that he recognized as guilt.

He passed the door of the room where his wife was sleeping, decided not to tell
her for the fourth time in three weeks, and went to brush his teeth.

He brushed his teeth looking in the mirror, the way you look, and then went to
lie down beside her, careful not to wake her.
