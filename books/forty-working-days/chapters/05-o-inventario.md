They started putting up the barriers on Thursday night and by Friday morning
they already reached the corner of Vetten.

Aurel watched from the window, coffee in hand. It was a low truck, with three
men, and the three of them worked well: one lowered the barrier, the next fitted
it, the third gave the pin one blow with a hammer. Fit, blow, three steps. Fit,
blow, three steps. He watched it for some ten minutes with the interest of a man
who likes to see work done properly.

Behind the truck came the thing that paints the line.

It is low and long, waist high, the color of dirty gray, and it runs on four
small wheels set inside its body, so that from a distance it seems to glide. At
the front it has a flat head that comes down almost to the asphalt and blows the
dust aside before the paint comes out; behind, the line comes out finished and
dry, its edge straight as if someone had used a ruler. It does about four
meters a minute and never changes speed, not even on the curve — on the curve it
turns its whole body on its own axis, without stopping, and the line follows.

Behind it came a fourth man, on foot, with a roller, doing the corners where it
could not get in. The fourth man took longer on each corner than the machine
took on a whole block. Nobody seemed to think this was a problem.

Then he went to settle the coffee and the kettle was already hot, because Friday
morning is coffee and he has done it forever, and since some point or other the
house does it with him.

---

His city had had a parade every year of his life, and he had gone to almost
all of them, and to most out of obligation.

As a boy, he was taken. That is something that happens to everybody and is
afterward remembered wrong: nobody *wants* to go to a parade at seven. You are
taken.

In 1972 his father took him and they arrived late.

That he remembers with a precision that makes no sense for something from
seventy-five years ago and makes perfect sense for something from seventy-five
years ago, because that is how it works: of the last ten years he remembers
little, and of that morning he remembers the cloth of his father's trousers.

They arrived late because his father was a man who ran late, and the sidewalk
was already four rows deep with people, and Aurel was seven years old and a
meter twenty tall and could see absolutely nothing. He saw backs. He saw the hem
of a lady's coat and a man's transistor radio, and for a few minutes he was
genuinely furious the way a child gets, without crying, jaw locked, determined
that this be put on record.

His father said nothing. He crouched, put his arms behind the boy's knees and
lifted, and set him on his shoulders with that maneuver fathers of that time
performed without warning and without asking, and the world rose a meter and a
half all at once.

And what Aurel saw from up there was not the parade.

It was the street.

He saw, for the first time in his life, a great many people at the same time.
Not the crowd as a mass — he saw **the heads**, one by one, going all the way to
the bend in the avenue and disappearing past it, and all of them turned the
same way. That is something a child has no way of seeing from the ground. From
the ground there is the lady with the coat and the man with the radio. From up
there there is a quantity of people that does not fit in a head, and all of
them, in that second, looking in the same direction.

He remembers thinking it was too big and liking it.

Then came the troops and he remembers the boots striking the asphalt. He
remembers that it was a single sound and that this frightened him in a way he
could not have explained. It is not fear of the soldier. It is something else.
It is the feeling, at seven, that many people can make the noise of one person.

He stayed on his father's shoulders until his father could not hold him any
longer, which was soon, because his father smoked two packs a day. He came down
and went back to seeing backs. And he went home at noon holding hands, hungry,
having seen almost nothing, and with the whole street put away inside him.

After that he went for work, which is a worse way of going. Twenty-some years of
going to parades with a notepad and getting back to the newsroom at two in the
afternoon, and the parade ceasing to be a parade and turning into four
paragraphs and a photo caption.

And it was in those years that he learned what it is for.

A parade is not for the enemy. That is the thing almost everyone gets wrong. The
enemy is not on the sidewalk. What goes down the avenue goes by for the people
who live there, and the message is not *we are able to defeat you*: the message
is *this exists, this is ours, and this is in order*. It is an inventory with
music. It is the State saying, once a year, out loud, on the main street: look
what we have.

Rome did it. It did it with the spoils going by on carts and the captives
walking in front, so that people would know how far the power had reached. And
it did it with the people on the sidewalk, not with any ambassador.

Aurel had known this since his early thirties and had never found it sinister.
He found it, at most, a little tacky. A State that needs to show what it has
once a year is a State that knows people forget, and that built-in modesty had
always struck him as almost endearing.

---

But what he remembers most about parades is the fight over the number.

Every year, on the following Monday, the paper published how many people had
been on the avenue. And every year the paper's number was different from city
hall's number, and the difference was enormous — forty thousand against a
hundred and eighty thousand, something like that — and there was a public
argument, a serious one, with methodology.

He revised a lot of those arguments. He knows how it was done: you divided the
area into quadrants, estimated the density per square meter, three people a
meter where it was tight, one and a half where it was loose, and multiplied.
There were people who did it with aerial photographs and a ruler. There was a
professor, Krall, who sent a letter to the newsroom every year correcting the
paper's arithmetic, and the paper published Krall's letter, and the next year
the arithmetic came out better.

That sounds small. Aurel spent thirty years thinking it was small — a squabble
over numbers between stubborn people, a second-page story, the business of a
retired professor.

Only, to fight over a number there has to be **the number**. One. One avenue,
one Saturday, a quantity of people who were there, which nobody knows for
certain, and which is therefore worth arguing about with method. The fight over
the number was serious people fighting over the same avenue.

He does not know how many people went to last year's parade. Not because he has
not looked — he looked, once, out of professional curiosity. He knows how many
*he* could have seen. There are numbers. There are a great many. There is no
fight at all, because there is nothing that needs to be decided in common.

The number exists with a precision that would have left Krall with nothing to
say. The avenue is counted in real time, person by person, and nobody has to
estimate density per square meter or take a ruler to any photograph. That is
why it is not published: an exact count is not a story. What gets published is
cuts, and the cuts are good, and each one is somebody's cut.

He still has one of Krall's letters, somewhere. Or he had.

He was an impossible man. He wrote by hand, on foolscap, in a tiny slanting
script, and he always began the same way — *Dear sirs, I return to the
subject* — and then came three pages of calculation, and then came the part
Márcio loved, which was Krall acknowledging, in one short dry paragraph, the
points on which the paper's estimate had improved since the year before.

He praised. A man who spent eighteen years correcting the same sum would stop
to say, in writing: on this point, sirs, you are right and I was wrong.

Krall is dead. That he knows because he looked.

Mira called at twenty past ten, which is not her time.

"Dad, real quick, I'm between one thing and another." She was in the car. She
is always in the car. "Nina wants to go tomorrow."

"To the parade."

"To the parade. And she wants to go with you. I said I'd ask, but I also said
you don't like crowds, so if you don't want to I'll take her and..."

"I'll go."

There was a half-second silence on the other end that he recognized, and that
hurt more than it should have, because it was a silence of surprise.

"You will?"

"I will."

"Dad, there'll be a lot of people. And you'll have to stand."

"I know what a parade is, Mira."

She laughed. It was a good laugh, and short, and she said okay, well then
great, and said Nina was going to lose her mind, and said she'd call later, and
said he should bring water and a hat, and hung up at ten twenty-three.

---

He stood in the kitchen with the phone in his hand.

He had not said yes for the parade. That he knew quite clearly and had no wish
at all to fool himself about. He was not going to the avenue to watch the State
show its inventory; he has seen too many inventories, and at this point in life
a man has the right not to go and watch anything go by.

He had said yes because a nine-year-old child, in a kitchen, on Thursday
afternoon, with a cookie in her hand, had said *like the parade*.

She had not been giving an answer. She had been answering something else, her
own thing, about fighting and about looking together, and she had solved the
problem with an ease that took him two days to admit was a real solution.

Everybody on the street looking at the same thing. And afterward you can fight
about whether it was good.

He went back to the window before putting the cup away.

The barriers now reached the corner of Vetten and there was nothing else there.
No name, no timetable, no notice hanging over the stretch, nothing offered to
anyone — only galvanized iron on a street, with three men and a truck, and the
chalk mark on the asphalt where the fitting goes. He stood looking at it and was
slow to understand what he had found strange, and what he had found strange was
that nothing was written.

Then he understood that it was just a street being made ready, and that streets
being made ready look like that, and he let the matter drop.

Aurel rinsed the cup.

He was not hopeful. It matters to him that this be said, even if only inwardly:
he had no hope at all, did not think an afternoon on the avenue was going to
give anything back to anyone, and suspected, with the whole experience of his
eighty-two years, that it would be three hours on his feet, in the sun, in the
noise, with a child wanting ice cream in the middle of it.

But he was going to be there.

He went to the bedroom and looked for the hat, which was where he thought it
was not, and put it on the chair, so as not to forget.
