She should have gone home.

She had no more shifts. She had a whole Friday on the floor by the counter, she
had the week's laundry, she had a man who was probably going to make lunch.

She took the 12 the other way.

---

The sidewalk outside the bakery was the same.

The awning, the grille rolled up, the dark stain in the gutter where the water
runs every day. It was Saturday, nearly ten, so the bakery was full and the
washer had already been put away, and there was a line of people buying bread
for the weekend with the kind of hurry that exists only in a bakery line.

Rita stood under the awning.

In the exact spot. She noticed she was looking for the exact spot and thought
it was an enormous piece of foolishness and stood in the exact spot anyway.

---

The building on the corner is the brick one with the crooked air-conditioning
boxes.

Fourth floor, third window.

It was closed.

---

She waited thirteen minutes.

She knew it was thirteen without needing to look, the way she knows the time,
and knowing the time while you wait for something that is not going to happen
is a small, private humiliation that she accepted as part of the price.

The window did not open. There was no reason for it to open. Nobody washes the
whole house on a Saturday morning; you wash on Friday, so the weekend is clean,
which is exactly what the person on the fourth floor had done eight days
before.

Nothing played.

---

And she stayed.

She stood under an awning, on a Saturday morning, with no music, with a
five-kilo bag on her shoulder and a little wooden box inside it, and what she
was doing there had no name at all and was good for nothing.

It was the first thing Rita had chosen in two weeks.

She thought that in those words, and it was not pretty, and it did not console
her. It was an observation with a bad taste: the first thing in two weeks that
she had decided to do with her own body for a reason of her own was to stand
still on a sidewalk waiting for something she had already understood was not
going to happen.

She stayed a little longer, only so that it would be she who decided when to
leave.

---

That was when she noticed the noise.

It came from below, from the direction of the avenue, and it was not street
noise. It was dense. There was a bass drum inside it — you could pick out the
drum, beating slowly — and over it something high that she could not place, and
under it that thick murmur of a lot of people together, which is like no other
sound in the world and which a person recognizes before knowing what it is.

And there were people coming up the street in the opposite direction from hers.

A family with two children. An old couple. A young man on his own with a closed
umbrella despite the sun.

All of them going the same way.

---

Rita settled the strap on her shoulder.

She knew about the parade. Everybody knew about the parade: there had been a
notice at the stop for the 12 for three weeks, the barriers had been going up
since Thursday, and the line was going to be diverted until two in the
afternoon, which was the only aspect of the matter that had anything to do with
her life.

She had not thought of going. She had not decided not to go, either. A parade
is something that happens in the city and not to you.

She looked at the fourth-floor window one more time.

Then she went down the street behind the family with the two children, without
having decided anything, the way she had arrived at everything else in those
two weeks.

On the Tuesday before, nothing came in.

The roster opens at six, and what comes, comes between six and a quarter past
seven. At twenty past seven Rita put the phone in her apron pocket without
having touched it once, which was something that had not happened in months,
because even in a bad week something comes in on a Tuesday.

She had decided on Sunday what she would do with a Tuesday like that.

---

The station is in Brenna, four stops past the street where the pavement ran
out, on the ground floor of a block. There is nothing written on the outside,
and nobody passing on the sidewalk could say what it is.

About three meters from the door it comes up: *home support, levels one and
two*, at shoulder height, a little in front of the plaster, with the hours
underneath in smaller letters. It walks with her to the door and disappears
when she goes in.

The other door is six meters away. It is wider and has no step.

Rita has never read what is written above the other door.

---

She brought the bag because bringing the bag is what she does, and halfway
there she noticed there was no reason to.

---

Inside there are twelve chairs in two rows, a water fountain, which works, and
a display with the ticket numbers, which is right.

"Registration number."

"Eighty-eight, twenty-two, fourteen."

Four more sat down after her, all from her modality, and nobody spoke to
anybody, which is not unfriendliness: whoever waits here is waiting for the
same thing and already knows what the other one would say.

She waited fifty-one minutes.

In that time her door opened four times. The other opened eleven. She counted
both and did nothing with the two numbers.

---

The young man at the station was in his early twenties, quick and polite in the
way of someone who has not yet grown tired of being polite.

The room is small, bright, with a table, two chairs and nothing on the wall. He
did not ask her to take anything off, did not ask her to put anything on, and
did not touch her with anything.

"Close your hand."

She closed it.

"Again, slowly."

She closed it slowly.

"Now keep still. Four minutes. You can look wherever you like."

She kept still for four minutes. He did not look at her once in those four
minutes, because there was nothing to look at.

---

"Grip within expected range for the operating interval," he said. "No
adjustment."

"It's been since July."

"Since July."

"There was a week in July when it wouldn't close. Then it came back. It didn't
come back the same."

He checked. It took long enough for her to understand that he was really
checking, and then he checked again, without hurrying, because he is good at
what he does.

"There's nothing on record."

"What do you mean, nothing?"

"There's no occurrence. Not in this cycle or the one before." He turned his
hand palm up, the way someone shows there is nothing in it. "It didn't go in
anywhere."

"I turned down two shifts."

"Turning down a shift doesn't generate an occurrence."

---

Her scheduled review is four months from now.

An adjustment outside the scheduled review requires authorization, and
authorization is not opened at the request of the provider. He said the two
things in the same order in which they are written, without dressing them up,
and said *four months* the way one says a date that is on paper.

"There's an update available," he said. "It's within your quota. Would you like
it, ma'am?"

"What is it?"

"It's the service layer. In the bottom corner, under what's left of the shift,
it'll also show the next address and how much time there is between one and the
other."

"All right."

It took forty seconds.

"A lot of people asked for it," he said.

---

At the door he said the deadline, the way you say something you say at every
door.

"If you'd like to respond, ma'am, the period is forty working days."

"Thank you."

---

It was twenty past ten.

On the 12, on the way back, she began putting it together again — the list, the
way she puts the list together under the awning — and the first item was no
longer there.

It was not that it had been wrong. She had understood perfectly: if there was
nothing on record, nobody had looked, and if nobody had looked, the hand had
never gone into anything, and something that had never gone into anything could
not have cost her two shifts in July, or the others after. The theory had not
been knocked down. It had been taken off the table.

What was left were the ones from before, and they are the usual ones: the
address, the lateness, the talking that counts for nothing anywhere. She had
carried those three since July thinking they were the worst, and now they were
the only ones, and she still had nowhere to hand in any of them.

She got off at her usual stop at ten past eleven, with the whole day ahead of
her.

---

In the first house after that, she looked at the bottom corner.

It was there, under what is left of the shift, the way he had said: a field for
the next address and one for the time between one and the other.

Both of them blank.
