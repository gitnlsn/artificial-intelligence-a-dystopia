She called at four thirty, from her room, lying on her stomach on the bed with
her feet in the air.

He takes a while to answer. Always. The call reaches him right away, the way it
reaches everybody, and even so he takes about five seconds — Nina counts the
five — and on the fifth he answers saying *hello* in a way nobody says hello
anymore.

"Grandpa, it's Nina."

"I know it's you."

"You didn't know."

"I did so."

"You say hello like you didn't know."

"I say hello because that's how you say it," he said.

This is an argument the two of them have had nine times, and Nina starts it on
purpose.

---

Grandpa is old-fashioned.

Nina likes that and has never had the slightest interest in understanding it.
It is like the smell of his apartment, or the drawer with receipts for things
from eighteen years ago, or the lamp in the corner — his living room raises the
light by itself at the end of the afternoon, like everybody's, and he switches
on the lamp over the top of it anyway, with a little screw knob that goes click,
and the light in the room comes down half a tone so as not to fight with his.

He writes by hand. He reads paper books and turns the page with a finger he
licks first, which is gross and which she loves. He sharpens pencils with a
razor blade, a real blade, which her mother thinks is incredibly dangerous.

He keeps plastic bags inside another plastic bag.

---

Slow he isn't.

Nina knows that because she has seen it. His house does everything her house
does and does it better, and he works it without looking, the way you work a
thing you have used for eleven years. In July her mother spent a whole
afternoon fighting with their house's delivery, which had been coming wrong for
three weeks, and Grandpa heard it from across the room, got up, fixed it in two
minutes and sat back down without saying anything.

Her mother was furious. Nina thought it was great.

---

"You're really coming tomorrow?"

"I'm coming."

"You promised."

"I'm coming, Nina."

"Because if you don't come I'll have to go with Mom and Mom keeps looking at her
phone."

"I'm coming."

---

She explained the plan three times.

The first time he followed. The second time he asked something she had already
said the first time, which was where Mom was going to drop the two of them off.
The third time he asked the same thing again.

Nina told him again.

She did not think anything about it. She did not make anything of it. She told
him again because he had asked, the same way she repeats the hello joke, and the
only thing that went through her head right then was that she had to say the
thing about the barrier before she hung up.

"Grandpa, we have to be at the barrier."

"What barrier?"

"The barrier at the front. By the curb. You can't be at the back because at the
back there's grown-ups and I can't see anything."

"We'll get there early, then."

"Nine thirty."

"Nine thirty is too early."

"Nine thirty," said Nina.

He laughed on the other end.

"Nine thirty."

---

"Bel's going to be in the band."

"Bel is the one who was suspended."

"Yeah. She came back."

"She came back because there's nobody else for the snare."

Nina opened her mouth.

She had told him that on Thursday. He had remembered the whole thing, with the
reason and everything, a week later — well, a day later, but for her a day after
something on a Thursday is a long way off.

"You remembered."

"Of course I remembered."

"Mom doesn't remember."

"Your mother has a lot on her mind."

"So do you."

"I don't have anything on my mind," he said. "That's why I remember."

---

She laughed and hung up at four fifty-one, because she had rehearsal at five and
she does not miss rehearsal.

Before she hung up:

"Grandpa."

"Yeah."

"Bring the hat."

"It's already on the chair."
