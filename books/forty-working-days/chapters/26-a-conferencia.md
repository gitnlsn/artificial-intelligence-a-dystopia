The quarterly review meeting is at ten, on the seventh floor, and lasts forty
minutes.

There are eleven people around a long table, and seven of them are in the room.
The other four are sitting the same way, at the same table, a little lighter
than the seven, and when someone passes between the table and the wall the
person passing does not disappear behind them — that is how you know which is
which, and nobody looks to find out. One of the four is from another city and
always arrives half a second late in everything they say, and for three years
nobody has mentioned it.

Voss has sat in the same place since 2039, which is the third from the left,
with his back to the window. Sitting with his back to the window is the reason
he turned down the window desk downstairs, and it is the same reason: material
against glare.

---

Brann presents.

She has run the section for three years, is in her early forties, is competent
and is quick, and Voss likes her the way he likes people: with no intimacy at
all and a good, stable opinion.

She went through the quarter's charts.

The charts sit in the middle of the table, lying flat on the top as though they
were printed on it, the size of a large sheet, and they turn slowly so that each
of the eleven gets the chart facing them. Volume, average handling time,
distribution by type. When Brann changes charts, all eleven change together, and
for about two tenths of a second the whole table goes blank.

The last chart is the reversal rate at the second instance. On that one, each
person sees their own name on the first line and the other ten in order below, and
the order is the same for everyone — it is the only chart of the quarter on
which the eleven people are reading exactly the same thing.

---

"Zero point four," said Brann. "Voss, zero point four."

Someone at the end of the table made an appreciative sound.

"The section average is two point one. And his, for the previous quarter, was
zero point six." She looked at him. "It's falling."

---

He explained.

He was not being modest and he was not making a scene. He explained because the
thing was wrong and he thought he ought to say so, and because in a room where
people work you say what you know.

"Reversal doesn't measure being right," he said.

Brann waited.

"The second instance applies the same table. If I apply the table correctly and
they apply the table correctly, the result has to be the same. The zero point
four isn't saying that I decide well. It's saying that the second instance and
I read the same line the same way."

"Which is what's expected," someone said.

"Which is what's expected," Voss agreed. "Only then the metric measures
consistency, not quality. A bad table applied consistently gives you zero point
four just the same."

Two seconds of silence.

And Brann smiled.

---

"That," she said, "is exactly why I wanted him to speak."

She turned to the table.

"Do you see? That's the rigor. He's suspicious of his own number. I'd like your
people to think like that."

---

Voss said nothing more.

He could have said it again, more slowly, in other words. He spent a few seconds
weighing it — weighing it seriously, the way he weighs a case — and decided not
to.

Because insisting would have turned into a scene. A second attempt, in front of
eleven people, after the director had reacted well, stops being technical
information and becomes a personal position, and a personal position at a
quarterly review meeting is something he disapproves of in anyone.

And because, checking inside himself honestly, he had no better metric to
propose.

That is the serious part and it is the part he took home. If reversal does not
measure being right, what does? He had thought about it before, and always
arrived at the same place: it would measure the outcome in the applicant's life,
and there is nobody who measures that, and for there to be someone you would
have to go after the people afterward, one by one, and ask what had happened to
them, and nobody in the world is going to pay for that.

So what is left is what can be measured.

---

At the end of the meeting Brann asked him to prepare a two-page note on his
method, to circulate on the floor.

He said he would.

---

In the afternoon he went back to his desk and the queue stood at twenty-nine.

He closed twenty-nine.

---

Only at night, on the bus, with the book shut on his leg, did he notice
something.

The praise was for consistency.

He had spent the whole day thinking he had been praised in spite of having said
that the metric was no good, and that was not it. He was praised *for* having
said it. His sentence had become proof of his quality. His distrust of the
number had become an attribute of the number.

He stayed with that for a while, looking out the bus window.

Then he opened the book again, because there were four stops to go and he has
read on the bus for twenty-some years, and because there was nothing to be done
with that thought, and because the two-page note was going to take work and he
would rather write it on Thursday morning, with a rested head.
