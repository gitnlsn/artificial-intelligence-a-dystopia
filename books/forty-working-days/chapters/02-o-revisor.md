He went looking in the morning, before coffee, still in his slippers, and it
took him forty minutes because he looked in the wrong drawer twice.

Forty minutes. Nothing else in his life takes forty minutes to find. He does
not lose a bill, does not lose a date, does not lose the name of a medicine,
does not lose the time of anything — he has never again had to remember a
single thing, and the thing turns up before he notices he was going to need it.
What he can still lose, and what he can therefore still look for, is paper.

It was in the bottom drawer of the desk, under the receipts from the 2029
renovation, in a clear plastic folder that had yellowed to the color of tea.
Inside the folder, a single rectangle of newsprint, cut out with scissors by
someone who was in no hurry: the four sides straight, the corners square.

Fifty-six-year-old newsprint is not fragile the way people imagine. It does not
crumble. It goes stiff. It loses its softness and takes on a dry weight, and
the color drifts toward tobacco, and if you fold it, it cracks instead of
creasing. Aurel did not fold it. He carried it to the living room in both
hands, in the slightly ridiculous way a person carries something that is not
heavy.

It was four lines, in six-point, at the foot of page eleven of the edition of
March 15, 1991.

> **ERRATUM** — In the edition of March 14, in the list of victims of the fire
> on Vetten Street, the name of Emil Roht was published. Emil Roht is alive. The
> victim was his brother, Aleks Roht. The paper regrets the error and apologizes
> to the family.

He knew it by heart. He even knew the type size. He read it anyway.

---

He was twenty-six in 1991 and had been in the newsroom for three years.

The list had come in at eleven forty at night, from the fire department, with
nineteen names and the apartment number beside each one, and the paper closed
at one. That was not a crunch: that was a Tuesday.

The smell was hot wax, fixer and reheated coffee, and it added up badly, and
after an hour you stopped smelling it. A light above the city desk had been
flickering for two years, and there was a note asking for it to be changed
pinned to the pillar, with the date.

None of that matters and he knows it does not matter. A whole building set up
to print the same sheet ninety-four thousand times is an expensive, clumsy
thing and nobody has any reason to miss it. What was being put together there
was not the sheet.

The revisers' desk was at the back, away from the window, and that was on
purpose: whoever revises should not see the street. There were four chairs and
that night two of them were taken. His and Márcio's.

---

The rule for the list was old and was written down nowhere, which at the time
did not seem like a problem.

A list of the dead is checked **by two**. One reads aloud, name by name, with
the apartment number; the other follows in the register with a finger and
answers *checks* or answers nothing. The one who reads is never the one who
follows. The reason is stupid and it is good: the eye that has read a name once
does not read the name again, it reads the memory of the name. Two people do
not have the same memory at the same time.

Márcio called it collation and said the word with a certain pleasure.

That night Halvar had taken the week off — his wife was having an operation,
something in her back — and Márcio was closing page three, which had come in
late and had a photo. A photo holds everything up. And the list from the
building on Vetten Street arrived at twenty past twelve with nineteen names,
and the city page closed at twelve forty-five.

Aurel looked over at Márcio, who had his back to him, bent over page three,
the ruler in his hand.

And he did not call him.

There was no deliberation. It is not that he weighed something and decided
wrong; it would be better if it were. He looked at the back of a busy man and
his body did the sum the body does, and he pulled up his chair and checked it
alone.

Alone goes like this: you read the name on the fireman's paper, you look for it
in the register, you find it, and the *find* is the problem. Alone you do not
read the register. You look in the register for the thing that is already in
your head, and the eye runs down the column waiting for that shape, and when
the shape appears the eye stops and says done. It is not reading. It is
recognition, which is a different operation and a much faster one.

Eleven minutes for nineteen names is thirty-five seconds per dead person. By
two, it came to nearly half an hour and there was no way to make it less.

In the building's register, at 402, it said *Roht, A.*

When he handed it in, Márcio looked at the clock and said, without raising his
head, *too fast for a list*. Aurel answered something that seemed reasonable at
the time.

Two brothers lived in apartment 402. The register had been filled in in 1986
and never updated, and the initial served for both of them, and the fire
department's list gave the full name of one of them, and the full name it gave
was wrong, because the fireman who wrote it down had asked a neighbor on the
sidewalk at three in the morning and the neighbor had said the name of the
brother he knew.

Aurel matched *Roht* against *Roht* and moved on.

That is it. There is nothing more. There was no pressure from above, no
contested headline, nothing that would make a good story afterward. A tired man
compared a surname with a surname and his eye did what the eye does, which is
to economize. The same economy that lets the *the* get across the turn of the
line and stay there fifty-seven years.

The paper went out in ninety-four thousand copies.

---

Emil Roht came into the newsroom at nine forty the next morning.

Aurel only found out because he was still there — he had slept two hours on the
sofa in layout, which people did — and because the girl from the front desk came
upstairs instead of phoning, which people did not.

He was a man of about forty, in a jacket, with the jacket buttoned. That was the
first thing Aurel saw and it is the one he has never managed to forget: the man
had dressed to come. He had woken up in someone else's house, because his own
had burned, and he had found a borrowed jacket, and he had buttoned the jacket
to walk into a newspaper building.

He did not shout. Aurel waited for the shout a good ten minutes, the way you
wait for something you think you have coming, and the shout never came.

What he said, standing, without taking the chair they offered him, was:

"My brother died yesterday. You printed it was me."

And then, because nobody answered anything:

"My mother lives out in the country. She reads this paper."

The city editor was a man called Vidor, who was rude to everyone every day and
who that morning stood up and came around the desk instead of talking across
it. He asked the brother's name. He wrote it down. He asked how it was spelled.
He wrote it down again. He asked the mother's name and where she lived and
whether she got the paper the same day or the day after.

The day after, said Emil Roht. Out there it comes the day after.

Vidor said the erratum would run in that day's edition, at the foot of an
odd-numbered page, which is where the eye falls, and that he would send someone
to take a copy out to the mother's town in the morning, by motorcycle, to get there
ahead of the truck. And he did. A motorcyclist called Peu went, and came back at
night, and charged a double day, and was paid.

Emil Roht thanked them. Aurel remembers finding it obscene that he should thank
them.

---

It was Márcio who wrote the erratum.

He spent nearly an hour on the four lines, which at the time seemed to Aurel an
old man's excess, and today does not. He tried some six versions. He could not
say *an error occurred*, because an error that occurs has no owner. He could not
say *incorrect information*, because the information had not come in incorrect
from anywhere — it had been checked wrong right there inside. He could not dress
it up, and he could not put the blame on the fireman either, who had worked all
night inside a burning building and had written down what a neighbor said.

What he ended up writing was: *Emil Roht is alive.*

Four words in the middle of the four lines, no adjective, no justification, no
regret in front. The regret came afterward and came small. First the statement.

Aurel asked whether his name, the reviser's, ought not to go with it. He was
twenty-six and thought he was being upright, and perhaps he was. Márcio said no.
He said an erratum is not a confession, it is a repair, and the one who signs
the repair is the paper, or else next time the man hides the mistake so as not
to sign.

Then he said the one thing Aurel took his whole life to understand:

"Our job is not to get it right. If it were getting it right, we'd have lost
already, and that's not the fight. Our job is to make sure it can be caught."

And he went back to his page.

---

Nothing happened to Aurel.

He spent the whole week waiting, and that is the part he never told anyone
because it does not come out well aloud. He waited to be called into Vidor's
office. He waited for a suspension, which was what they gave. He even thought
about the order in which he would sell things if he were let go, which at
twenty-six is a kind of vanity.

He was not called. Vidor went past him on Thursday and talked about something
else. Márcio never brought it up again — never, in nine years, until he
retired — and it took Aurel some fifteen years to understand that this had been
on purpose and that it was the most generous thing anyone had ever done for him.

What happened was that he never again checked a list alone. Not when they were
short-handed, not when the page was late, not when the man across the desk
huffed. Thirty-four years. He called someone, and if there was nobody, he held
the list back and let the page close without it, and once that cost him a
serious dressing-down and once it cost a footnote saying the list would appear
the next day.

That is what was left of the episode inside the trade. It is little and it is
everything. Nobody ever wrote the rule down anywhere, and it existed for
thirty-four years inside one man only because another man in a buttoned jacket
had climbed a flight of stairs.

---

About the mother he never knew.

That is the splinter that stayed. Peu rode his motorcycle to her town, left at
eleven in the morning, and came back at night, and charged a double day, and
Vidor paid without arguing and without asking anything more than *delivered?*,
and Peu said *delivered*, and left.

Delivered to whom, nobody asked. Whether into her hand or into the mailbox.
Whether she had already seen the previous day's paper, which arrived the day
after, which is to say that same day, possibly that same morning, possibly
before. Whether someone in the family had got to her first by phone. Whether
she spent an hour with it, or a whole night, or not a minute.

Aurel thought about it more often than he would like to admit and always came
to the same place, which is no place. The erratum existed. It ran at the foot of
an odd-numbered page. A man on a motorcycle took a copy. It is all verifiable
and it proves nothing about the one thing that mattered, which was a woman in a
small town, on a March morning in 1991, with a newspaper in her hand.

What the counter gives is not the repair. It is the chance of the repair, and
the chance is still an enormous thing, and it takes a certain age not to find
it small.

---

Aurel stood with the clipping in his hand for a long time, with the sun already
high and the coffee not made.

Fifty-six years, and he had misunderstood what he was keeping.

He had thought his whole life that he kept it as a punishment — the card you
carry in your pocket so as not to forget what you are capable of. Many revisers
keep one. It is almost a habit of the profession, and it is an honest habit.

But that was not it. He stood with the paper in his hand and saw, with a late
and uncomfortable clarity, that you keep an object for what it *proves*, and
what that rectangle proved was not that Aurel had failed.

It proved that you could get there.

A man in a buttoned jacket had climbed a flight of stairs, at nine forty in the
morning, and had stood in front of a man called Vidor, and had said a sentence
of nine words, and the world had said back *that's right, we'll undo it*. In
eighteen hours. No form. No protocol number. No deadline. A man, a stairway, a
sentence, and on the other side another man with a name, with a desk, with the
obligation to answer right there, with his own face.

What had been lost was not the common record. That he had already understood
the day before, sitting in the armchair, and had thought it was the bottom.

What had been lost was the **counter**.

The clipping is paper and so it is still a clipping. It was printed ninety-four
thousand times on one night in March and the ninety-four thousand were the
same, and the one that is left is in his hand, and says today what it said
then, and would say the same thing to whoever came. It is not nostalgia: it is
a physical property of a thing that cannot be adjusted afterward.

Aurel put the clipping back in the folder and the folder on top of the desk, not
in the drawer, and stood looking at it from across the room.
He was trying to think of a place, just one, in the whole city, where a person
could climb a flight of stairs and stand in front of another person and say
*what is written here about me is wrong* — and receive, on the same day, from
someone with a face, the answer that yes, it was, and that it would be unsaid.

The kettle went cold. He did not make the coffee.
