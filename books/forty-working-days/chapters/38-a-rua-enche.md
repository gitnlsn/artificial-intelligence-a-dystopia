They got there at nine twenty-two and the barrier was already taken.

Nina saw it from a distance and her stomach dropped.

"Mom. Mom. There's already people at the barrier."

"There's room, Nina."

"Not at the barrier!"

---

There was, a little farther down, past the corner, and they walked about eighty
meters and found a stretch with two open gaps.

Her mother put Grandpa on Nina's right, without Nina having to ask, and then
said she was going to stay at the back because she had something to see to, and
stayed at the back, about four meters behind, leaning against the wall.

---

Grandpa had his hat on.

He kept both hands on top of the barrier, one on each side, the way of someone
who has stood a long time on his feet many times and knows where to lean.

"We timed it right," he said.

"Nine thirty."

"Nine twenty-two."

"Nine thirty is what I said and we got here before," said Nina.

"That isn't the same thing."

"It's almost."

---

The street filled quickly between nine thirty and ten.

It filled in a way Nina found interesting to watch: people arrived from behind,
kept pushing forward until they bumped into whoever was already there, and
stopped. And then more arrived and stopped behind those. In about twenty
minutes there were eight or nine rows at her back and you couldn't walk anymore.

There was a smell of corn. There was someone selling water from a cart and
shouting the price. There was a child crying somewhere behind, with that cry of
a child who is tired in the morning, and Nina recognized the kind of cry and
felt sorry for the child's mother.

---

She looked both ways.

Both ways it was people all the way to the curve.

On one side it went as far as the pharmacy corner and turned; on the other it
went down until she couldn't make out faces anymore, only heads, and the heads
kept going after that, and all of them were turned the same way, which was up
the avenue, where the things were going to come from.

Nina looked at that for a few seconds.

She thought it was pretty.

And she thought it was normal, the way you think a full stadium is normal, or a
supermarket line on a Sunday: that's how it gets when a lot of people go to the
same place.

She didn't think anything more about it.

---

Grandpa was quiet.

"Grandpa."

"Hm."

"There's a lot of people."

"There are."

"Have you ever seen this many people?"

He took a little while.

"I have."

"When?"

"Right here. On this street."

"When you were a kid?"

"When I was a kid and after that too." He straightened his hat. "This happens
every year, Nina."

"I know it happens every year."

"You'd never come."

"I came when I was little."

"You were three. You slept."

---

At five past ten a noise began to come from up the street.

Not the music yet. A noise of something getting organized: a megaphone far off,
a whistle, and the sound of a great many people at once making that *oh* a crowd
makes when something appears at the curve and you can't see yet what it is.

Nina put a foot on the bottom bar of the barrier, stepped up, and held on tight.

"Nobody can see anything yet," said Grandpa.

"I'm getting ready."
