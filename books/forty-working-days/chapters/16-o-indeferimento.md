She went because the morning was left over.

That is the truth and it is a slightly ridiculous truth: if Mr. Vilmar had been
in his house at a quarter past seven, Rita would not have gone to Block C that
Thursday, nor probably on any other day, because to go to Block C you need a
whole morning with no use for it, and she had not had a whole morning with no
use for it since 2044.

---

Block C has a line outside that moves fast and a line inside that does not move.

The one outside is triage. A young man in a vest asks your business and types
it, and the machine prints a ticket with a letter and three numbers, and the
letter is what matters. Rita got G-114.

She asked what the G was.

"Allocation review," said the young man, without looking up.

"Is it a lot?"

"It's quiet today."

It was nine fifty and Rita was called at eleven twenty-six.

An hour and thirty-six minutes, and it was not a line for a busy window. All
four were open and none of them had anyone in front of it for more than six or
seven minutes.

The line inside does not move because what you are waiting for there is not the
window. The window takes four minutes. The rest is waiting for a person to look,
and a person has hours and has a quantity, and is the only thing in that
building that does.

---

The waiting room at Block C is good.

She noticed that and was bothered at having noticed it. It is clean, it has air,
it has high-backed chairs and not the plastic kind, it has cold water at three
places in the room and none of them with a line, and a restroom that was clean
at ten in the morning on a Thursday. There is a display that shows the ticket
and the window, and the display is always right.

Almost nobody in the room is in the room.

A young man moves his hand, small, in his lap, and about forty centimeters from
his chest there is something open in three columns that he pushes aside with two
fingers. An older woman laughs at a rectangle the size of a tray, hanging at the
height of her face, tilted a little, as if it were resting on a table that does
not exist. A man in a suit answers someone half a second late, and the someone
is in the empty chair beside him, or is where the empty chair is.

From where Rita sits, what you see is the back of all of it, which is gray and
smooth and has nothing on it, some nine or ten gray shapes standing still in the
air of a waiting room. Nobody finds this worth noticing. Neither does Rita.

---

Beside her sat an older woman who opened nothing.

The woman changed position six times in the first half hour. She crossed her
leg, uncrossed it, put her bag on her lap, took the bag off her lap, looked at
the display, looked at the door, got up to drink some water and came back, and
got up again ten minutes later and went nowhere.

Rita noticed all six. She is good at it — it is half her job, noticing when
someone is restless before they say so.

The display is on the front wall, above the door to the windows. It is long, the
size of a door laid on its side, with the tickets in one column and the window
number in the other, and it is flat on the wall itself — paint, not air. When a
ticket changes, the whole line slides up one place and the one below comes in.
It makes no sound.

Rita keeps looking at the display, which is the only thing in that room written
the same for everyone, and that is why she looks at it.

She has waited in far worse places to get far smaller things.

There is nothing wrong with Block C. Everything Rita is going to feel that day
she will feel inside a well-kept building, with good air, having been treated
politely by every person she spoke to.

---

Window seven had a woman of about fifty named Idalina, according to her badge,
and Idalina was good to her.

She heard Rita out, all of it. She did not interrupt. When Rita began to explain
the business of the nineteen steps — because she told it, she had been carrying
that sentence of thirty-some words for five days and at last she had an ear in
front of her — Idalina listened, and at the end she made a face that Rita
recognized at once, because it is the face Rita herself makes in people's
houses.

It is the face of someone who understands and is not going to be able to do
anything.

"Honey," said Idalina, "I'm going to log it. But I need to tell you something
first."

"Go ahead."

"I don't decide anything here."

---

She explained it with the patience of someone who has explained it many times.

The window logs it. What is logged goes into a queue. The queue is reviewed. The
review sends back a decision, and the decision comes back to the display, and the
display calls the ticket. She, Idalina, does not see the decision being made and
does not know who makes it and cannot call anybody.

"I used to be able to," she said. "I had an extension."

"And now?"

"Now I've got this," and she tapped the keyboard with two fingers, with no anger
at all, the way you tap the desk you work at. "And I type well, you know. I type
everything a person says."

She typed everything Rita said. It took almost four minutes. Rita watched, from
the side, the screen filling up.

And then Idalina said *there* and pressed a key and told her to sit down, it
would be quick.

---

It was quick.

Nineteen minutes.

---

::: {.registro}
Request G-114/10-17. Review of allocation criteria. Applicant: home caregiver,
Reg. 88-2214.

Claim: the lateness occurrence recorded on 10/12 resulted from assistance
provided outside the scope of the shift.

Analysis: the assistance described is not listed as a duty of shift 4471-B. The
lateness record reflects the time actually verified and is correct. There is no
material error to correct.

Shift scope and distribution criteria are service parameters, not individual
decisions, and are not subject to review at the request of the provider.

Denied.

Period for a new request on the same matter: 40 working days.

Review: Voss, M. — 0417
:::

---

Rita read it twice, standing, beside the display.

There was no error. That is the thing. She looked for a sentence that was wrong
and there was none: she had arrived late, the time is right, going down nineteen
steps is not a duty of the shift, and never was, and she knew that when she went
down them.

*There is no material error to correct.*

Perfectly right. There is not.

What she wanted to say was not that there was an error. What she wanted to say
was that something had happened and the thing had a reason, and that she would
like the reason to be written somewhere together with the lateness, so that the
two things would be read together by someone, once, some day.

It took her a few seconds to realize that this had happened. The reason was
written. Idalina had typed all of it, with care, in four minutes.

And it was read. And it was answered in nineteen minutes, correctly, in writing,
politely, and the answer was that it was not subject to review.

---

At the bottom there was a name.

*Voss, M. — 0417*

She read it. She ran her eye over it and read it, the way you read the footer of
any document, together with the number, as if the name and the number were the
same kind of thing — two items of identification on a despatch.

She did not think: there is a person called Voss.

She did not think of an age, or a face, or a room, or whether he had read the
part about the nineteen steps or only the claim. She thought nothing, because
there is nothing to think about a footer.

She put the paper in the outside pocket of the bag, folded in three, where she
keeps prescriptions.

---

She left Block C at eleven fifty-one and stood on the front steps for a moment
because of the sun.

And what frightened her — frightened her truly, and she thought about it that
night lying down, and thought about it again weeks later — was that she was not
angry.

Not in the least.

Her shoulder was aching on the side of the bag, and she was working out how many
days forty working days are, which comes to almost two months, and concluding
that it was all right. That she could wait. That it was not the end of the
world.

That was why she stood still on the steps.

Because a person who comes out of a public building with a paper that says no is
usually angry, and anger is good for something: anger is what makes you tell
other people, and look for who else has been through it, and go back. Rita had
been angry once at a health plan, on someone else's behalf, and won.

And there, on the steps, with the sun in her face, she noticed that she felt
nothing but a well-managed tiredness, and that she had been treated politely
from beginning to end, and that everyone was right about everything, and that
she had no one to fight and nothing to fight about.

She went down the steps. She had a little over an hour before the one o'clock
shift.
