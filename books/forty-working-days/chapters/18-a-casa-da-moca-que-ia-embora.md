Juno is twenty-six, with an external fixator on her right leg and an amount of
energy that does not fit in that apartment.

It was a motorcycle. Not hers — she was on the back, which she tells with a
particular anger, because *it wasn't even my bike*, and because the guy who was
driving is perfectly fine and has texted twice.

The fixator comes off in three months. Then it's physical therapy. Then,
according to Juno, it's the airport.

It is metal and has eight pins and she hates it with an intimacy Rita
respects.

There is something better. Juno knows what it is, knows its name, said the name
once — and said, in the same sentence and without changing her tone, that it
was not her case.

The better thing is not a device. It is something they put inside, and the bone
closes on its own in five weeks, and the person walks out with the whole leg and
nothing on the outside. Rita has seen two. One was on a boy from Kalden who fell
off a bicycle, and the boy had three stitches on his thigh the size of a
fingernail and nothing else, and fifteen days later he was sitting on the
living-room floor with his legs crossed.

Juno has eight pins and gets out in three months. It was not a complaint. It
was information, the way you say the price of something you are not going to
buy.

Rita did not ask why. In the houses she goes into, what exists and what arrives
are two different subjects, and nobody confuses the two, and nobody talks about
it.

---

She is the only one of Rita's houses that is not an old person's.

That changes everything, and it took Rita about three visits to adjust. In an
old person's house the body is the subject: what hurts, what won't bend, what
we're going to manage today. Here the body was a nuisance. Juno talked about the
fixator the way you talk about construction in the building — irritating,
temporary, with a date.

And she talked about everything else all the time, loudly, with something
running soundlessly in a corner of the wall that she didn't even look at.

---

The shift is eight to eleven and it is the easiest of Rita's week. Dressing the
pins, which Juno nearly does by herself now, dorsiflexion exercises, a shower
with the stool, and the rest is presence.

Presence is what the roster lists as *support*.

In practice it means three hours on her feet, because Rita does not sit in a
client's house — it is no one's rule, it is hers, and it has been twelve years
that way, and not once has she wanted to break it, and at the end of the day she
does not feel those three hours any more than she feels the others. Three hours
in a one-bedroom with a woman of twenty-six who cannot go out, and who will go
out of her mind if Rita stays quiet.

---

That Friday Juno had the map.

Not a real map — it's on the screen, but she calls it the map, and she turns the
screen toward Rita all the time, and Rita looks, and Rita never knows what to do
with it.

Turning the screen is something you do on purpose and almost nobody does it.
Juno always does. She does it with the map, she does it with the photo of her
cousin, she does it with the house her cousin says they can rent, and she has
never asked whether Rita wanted to see. It is the reason Rita likes her more
than she should, and she knows it is the reason, and finds the reason a little
silly.

"Here," she said. "This one. Twenty-two degrees all year. All year, Rita."

"Where is that?"

"Far."

"How far?"

"Very." She laughed. "Like, very very."

Juno's cousin has lived there for four years and says they need people. Juno
is going with the insurance money, if it comes through. She is going in five
months, if the leg lets her. She is going to stay two years and then see.

She tells it three times a week with small variations, and Rita knows the plan
better than she knows her own sister's plans.

---

Here is something Rita has never said and thinks every time:

Juno's plan is good.

It is not a sick person's fantasy. The cousin exists, the work exists, the
insurance money will come through because the claim has already gone through,
and Juno is twenty-six and has a technical diploma and no children and no sick
mother. She is going to make it.

And there is not one thing in that plan that Rita can use.

She is not envious. She has measured this honestly, because she was raised to
think envy a dirty thing and has spent her life checking whether she had any. It
is not envy. It is that she sits in that one-bedroom three times a week and
spends three hours inside a future, and the future is good, and the future is
not hers in any sense — not by identification, not by hope, not by that thing
people say about *seeing your children go further*, because Juno is not her
daughter, she is a young woman Rita has known for seven months who in five
months will be gone and will stop texting after the second month.

She will like it. And it will end.

---

At twenty past ten Juno asked.

It came out of nowhere, in the middle of something else, the way good questions
come.

"Rita."

"Yeah."

"What did you want to be?"

Rita was rolling up the bandage.

"What do you mean?"

"Like, before. When you were my age. What did you want to be?"

---

She was going to say *oh, I always liked looking after people*.

That sentence exists, it is true enough, it closes the conversation gracefully,
and Rita has used it some forty times in her life, including in interviews.

And that morning she looked at Juno and did not use it. Not by decision. She
opened her mouth and the other thing came out, and afterward she was a little
frightened that it had.

"A crime scene investigator."

Juno let out a shout of laughter.

"What?!"

"A crime scene investigator." Rita was laughing too, and had stopped folding the
towel halfway. "I was seventeen."

"Why?!"

"Because of a show." She shrugged. "But it wasn't only the show. I liked that
thing of looking at something quiet and the quiet thing telling you what
happened. A room. A shoe. A glass on the table. Nobody telling you anything,
just what's there."

"That's kind of beautiful."

"It is, isn't it?"

"And then?"

"And then I was seventeen, and my mother got sick that year, and I did the
aide course because it was six months and it paid, and the idea was to do the
other one after."

"And the after?"

"The after didn't come."

---

Juno was quiet for a few seconds, which for her is a lot.

"You'd be good."

"Oh, come on."

"No, listen." She pointed her chin at her own leg. "You look at this leg and you
know what happened this week. You say it before I do. Last week you said I'd
slept on my side."

"It was marked on the sheet."

"**Exactly**," said Juno.

And Rita laughed and changed the subject, because it was already past half past
ten and there was still the shower.

---

On the 12, heading to the second house, she stayed with it.

Not feeling sorry for herself. She does not do that and has a horror of doing
it.

What stayed was smaller and stranger: it had been some fifteen years since
anyone asked her what she wanted to be.

And she noticed that she had answered fast. Without thinking, without searching.
The answer was there whole, with the show, and the shoe, and the glass on the
table, and the reason — the reason was there, worded, as if she had rehearsed
it.

A thing you keep ready for fifteen years is a thing you were hoping someone
would ask.
