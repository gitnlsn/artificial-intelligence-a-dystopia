He shaved, which was not necessary, and changed his shirt, which was less
necessary still.

He noticed both things in the elevator, already going down, and undid neither.

---

The 41 takes four stops and he stood as far as the third, because sitting and
getting up again is worse than staying up. Solvig Street has a ramp in place of
the three steps it used to have, and the ramp is good: wide, with a handrail on
both sides, and with that rough surface that is not pretty and that works.

He noticed the ramp the way he used to notice a well-laid-out page. Things in
that city were very well made.

On the third floor the waiting room was full.

That surprised him, and it took him a while to understand why — because he had
come expecting an empty corridor, and what there was was people. About thirty
of them. An old woman with her middle-aged son, and the son holding two coats.
A very large man in a small chair. A young woman with her leg stretched out in
front of her, in a metal frame from the knee down, reading something on a
screen with the face of someone who is not reading.

Nobody was talking, and it was not a hospital silence. People were busy.

The middle-aged son had three blocks of text standing still in the air a little
above his mother's shoulder, at the height where you would put a shelf, the
size of business cards and turned toward him alone — Aurel could see the back
edge of all three, which is gray and has nothing on it. One of them changed by
itself every two minutes. The man read without moving his head and sometimes
answered his mother half a second late.

The young woman in the frame was laughing to herself at something that sat
just above her bad knee, and that from where he was looked like a pale
rectangle with no thickness, the size of a plate.

A man at the back was moving his mouth without sound, and in front of him, at
chest height, there was a column of short lines that went up one line each time
he stopped.

Aurel could see where every one of them was and could not see what was written
on any of them. It had always been that way and it had never bothered anyone.
What you see from outside is the place.

The air was good. The chairs had high backs. There was a water fountain, and
the water fountain worked.

Aurel sat at the end of a row and took the paper out of his pocket.

---

He had printed it. This is a thing he does and that Mira finds
incomprehensible: when something that matters arrives, he puts it on paper,
because paper does not change afterward. He had printed it the day it came and
folded it in three, and the paper had already gone soft at the fold from being
checked so often.

::: {.registro}
Notice 2208-K. Beneficiary Reg. 39-1187.
Functional assessment of 08/12 records loss of sensation and intermittent
claudication in left lower limb, with indication for pain-management implant.
Outpatient procedure. Estimated duration: 40 minutes.
Booking confirmed for 10/18, 14:30 — Unit 3, third floor.
The booking may be canceled by the beneficiary up to 24 hours in advance,
without prejudice to a new indication. No companion is required.
:::

He read it again, in the middle of the full room, with the paper resting on his
leg.

It was all correct. It had been correct since it arrived and he checked it
anyway, the way you check.

---

The assessment had been two months earlier and he had not gone anywhere to have it.

It went like this: he went to a routine appointment, which really was routine,
and at the end of it all someone — not a person, a notice — asked whether he
would like to add a functional assessment, which took twelve minutes and could
be done right there.

The notice stayed still half a meter from him, at chest height, the size of a
small sheet of paper, with the question at the top and two words at the bottom,
one on each side. *Now* and *another time*. It did not blink and it had no
deadline written on it. When he turned his face to the wall, it stayed where it
was, and when he looked back, it was the same size, in the same place.

He said yes because he was already sitting down.

They did not ask why. They did not need to: whoever had suggested it already
knew about the leg.

Twelve minutes. A short corridor, with a pale matte floor, with a black mark
painted in the middle and nothing else — no device on the wall, nothing on the
ceiling you could point to. He walked from one end to the mark, sat, stood up,
sat. The floor did nothing he could notice; it was a floor.

A young woman who did not look at him once said *you can go*. It did not hurt
at all. They did not ask him anything he had to answer in writing.

And that was not an examination. He understood it at once and said nothing,
because there was nobody to say it to and because he had no objection to make.

What the twelve minutes measured was not the leg. It was the hesitation: the
four tenths of a second longer that his left foot spends on the floor before the
right one leaves. Four tenths. It had been two, eighteen months before, and he
had not known that, and the corridor of his own house had known it since last
winter.

What the assessment did, then, was something else, and it took him a few
nights to put it precisely: it took a thing that had been observed and turned
it into a thing that was written. While it was only observed it existed for
nobody — you could not ask for anything with it, you could not appeal against
it, you could not show it to anyone. Twelve minutes in a corridor with a mark
on the floor, and the same thing came to exist in a field, with a date.

Three weeks later the notice came with the date already inside it.

That is the part he examined a few times lying down, at night, with the
honesty he keeps for when there is nobody nearby to defend himself against:
**he did not ask.** He did not write to anyone, did not explain anything to
anyone, did not have to convince a single person that his leg was worth forty
minutes. There was no line. There was no deadline. A date came and the date was
good, and there was a way to cancel it, and the way to cancel it was easier
than booking would have been.

He was eighty-two and had a leg that tingled from the knee down after twenty
minutes, and the world had noticed it before he did.

He did not find that sinister. He found it easy, which is a worse word, and he
knew it.

---

He did not tell Mira.

The reasons were three and he had them in order, because a man who has spent
his life with text does not leave a decision without an argument. First: she
would want to come, and coming meant leaving a meeting, and he would spend the
whole afternoon knowing she had left a meeting. Second: it said right there on
the paper that no companion was required, and the paper was correct.

The third took him longer to put into words and he put it into words anyway: if
she knew, she would not let him go to the parade on Saturday.

She would say it was madness, and she would be technically right, and she
would call someone and check, and at the other end someone would say that the
recommendation is to avoid standing for long periods in the first few days. And
then there would be no way.

He had told his granddaughter he was going. He had not told her in a way that
admitted revision.

---

They called his name at twenty to three, which is twenty minutes after the
time, and he thought that reasonable.

The one who called was a young woman of about thirty, in a pale blue uniform,
who said his name with his surname and checked his date of birth and waited
for him to answer all of it before she lowered her clipboard.

"Did you come alone, sir?"

"I did."

"Is there someone at home tonight to look after you?"

He thought about saying yes. He said that he lived alone.

She noted that down with no change at all in her face, and said that he would
stay under observation for forty minutes afterward, and that if he felt
anything at all he should just say so, and he noticed that she spoke quickly in
the way of someone who has three rooms.

He noticed too that she was the second person in that building who had spoken
to him, and that the first had been the young woman at the water fountain
saying it was out of cups and she would be right back.

---

The room was small and bright and there was nothing wrong with it.

An adjustable table in the middle, with a paper cover; a long-armed lamp on the
ceiling; a device standing against the wall on the right, the size of a tea
cart, with two arms folded inward the way an insect folds its legs when it is
still. There was a chair in the corner, for the companion he had not brought.

It smelled of nothing. That bothered him a little, and it took him a while to
locate why, and he located it: hospitals had always smelled. The newsroom had
smelled. A thing with no smell is a thing nobody has worked in.

The young woman asked him to take off his trousers and lie down and covered his
right leg with a paper sheet, and said she would be right back, and came back
once to check something that did not involve touching him, and went out again.

Aurel lay looking at the ceiling.

---

And then the voice spoke, and it spoke from his left side, at the height of his
head, at a volume that was exactly correct.

"Good afternoon. You are Aurel, registration number 39-1187?"

It took him two seconds.

"I am."

"Do you confirm your date of birth?"

He confirmed his date of birth.

"I am going to describe what will happen before we begin. You may interrupt me
at any point, and you may withdraw at any point until the start."

And it described it.

---

It took nearly six minutes and Aurel heard all of it.

He heard it the way he used to hear a proof. It is not something he decides to
do; it is what happens to a man who spent thirty-four years with a page in
front of him and the obligation to let nothing through. The ear goes ahead of
the attention, looking for the place where the sentence promises one thing and
delivers another.

He looked. He was looking the whole time.

The voice said where the implant would go and said why there. It said how long
it took. It said he would feel pressure and not pain, and then it said — and
this he noticed, because it was the part where the lie usually comes — that
some people did feel pain, and that the proportion was small, and it said the
proportion, and said that if he felt it, he should say so, and that there were
things that could be done.

It said the effect was not immediate. That the sensation in the leg would
change over some weeks and that the change could be unpleasant at first. It did
not say *discomfort*. It said unpleasant.

---

And it said how he had come to be there.

This Aurel had not expected, and it was the part he listened to more closely
than any other, because it was the only one where he did not know what was
coming.

The indication had not come from his leg. It had come from a comparison:
eleven years of crossings of a corridor, eighty-two years of age, a night
interval that had shortened, a hesitation that had doubled in eighteen months —
and, on the other side, a large number of people whose crossings had changed in
the same way and in the same order. Of those, a known proportion had stopped
leaving the house within eighteen to thirty months.

The voice said the proportion. It said too how many people there were in the
comparison, and the number was high, and Aurel found the number high.

And then it said, without his having asked, the following: that the proportion
is a property of the group and not a property of him. That there is no way of
knowing, beforehand, whether he is inside it or outside. That the procedure is
offered to everyone in that comparison because there is no way to separate the
ones who need it from the ones who do not, and that this is a limitation of the
method and not information about his case.

It said that last sentence whole, in those words, and then moved on to the next
item.

It said the implant could not be removed without a second procedure and said
what that second procedure consisted of, without his having asked.

It said what it did not know. It said that there were no data on how the
implant behaved after twelve years, because the instrument was nine years old.

Aurel stayed with that.

*Because the instrument was nine years old.* Not *we are still following it*.
Not *the results are promising*. The instrument was nine years old, and so there
were no data for twelve, and the sentence stopped there and did not go looking
for comfort anywhere.

Márcio would have liked that sentence. It was the thought that came, whole, and
he did nothing with it.

---

There was no mistake.

He checked the whole thing and there was none. No word promising what the next
did not deliver. No loose number. No adjective doing a verb's work. No *the* at
the turn of the line.

Aurel lay on an adjustable table, with the paper sheet on his right leg,
searching the speech of a machine for the place to mark, and did not find it.

And then came the other thing, which was the real one, and which arrived slowly
the way the worst ones arrive:

If there had been.

If there had been one wrong word there, just one — a proportion rounded down, a
deadline stated with room to spare, a *may* where the right word was *will* —
where would he have marked it.

There was no margin. There was no page. There was no print run, there was no
next edition, there was no man in a buttoned jacket climbing a staircase at
nine forty in the morning because what was written about him was wrong. The
sentence had not been published. It had been said to him, there, once, at the
right volume, and it did not exist anywhere else in the world at that second.

An erratum needs three things, and he knew all three by heart.

The job is not to get it right. The job is to make it possible to be caught.

This was correct and it could not be caught, and the two things had no
relation to each other at all, and it was the first time in his life that the
two things had had no relation to each other at all.

---

"Do you have any questions before we begin?"

He was eighty-two, with four thousand books read and thirty-seven thousand
pages revised.

"No," he said.

"Do you authorize the start?"

"I authorize it."

---

There was a movement to his right, and the device standing against the wall
unfolded one of its arms, unhurried, with the sound of something well oiled.
The lamp on the ceiling came down fifteen centimeters and turned.

Aurel looked at that and then looked at the ceiling again, because looking was
not going to help at all and he had already seen work being done by people who
knew how to do it.

His left leg was cold where they had wiped something on it.

And it was then, lying there, with his hands on his stomach, that he spoke —
out loud, the way he speaks out loud, because a sentence that does not stand up
out loud does not stand up:

"Could you put on some music?"

---

"Which music would you like?"

He opened his mouth.

The house put on music every day. It had since 2036. It was always good and he
had never needed to choose any, and so he had never needed to know the name of
any. The name he had was older than that, and it was only one.

A yellow-sleeved record that Ilse used to put on. He did not know the name of
the record or the name of whoever sang; he knew two words because she
used to say them before she put it on, and he knew the name of the band because
it was written on the sleeve and he read everything.

"Great Divide," he said. "Cardigans."

Three words in the other language, said aloud in a small room. He said the last
one as it is spelled, which is how he had learned it.

There was no confirmation.

---

It started low and rose to a point he did not need to choose.

A woman singing slowly, without forcing anything. A guitar doing the same thing
about four times before it changed. A big, soft bass underneath everything, and
the woman singing in another language, one he does not speak and knows from a
long way off, and the whole thing had a sadness that was not apologizing for
being sadness.

At the volume at which it reached him you could hear that the voice did not
climb. There was a place, halfway through, where another singer would have
climbed. She did not, and nothing came to fetch the music.

He did not need to put it anywhere. It was already put.

It was beautiful. It was exactly beautiful.

Aurel lay listening with his eyes open, with his left leg cold and the arm
coming down slowly at his right side, and at some point during the third time
the guitar did the same thing before it changed, he stopped being there.
