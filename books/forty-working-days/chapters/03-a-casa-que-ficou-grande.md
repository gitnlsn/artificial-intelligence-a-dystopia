At ten past three he woke, the way a man of eighty-two wakes, for no reason and
without sleepiness, and lay in the dark working out how long he could put it
off.

Five minutes, he decided. Then six. Then he sat on the edge of the bed and
pushed his feet into his slippers.

The hallway was warm.

Not the living room, not the bedroom: the hallway. That strip of floor between
the bedroom and the bathroom, two and a half meters, was at a temperature
pleasant to a bare foot, and it was like that because he crosses it between
three and half past three almost every night since his prostate decided to
have an opinion, and the house had learned this on its own at some point last
winter.

The house knows other things.

It knows how often he gets up, and how long it has been that way, and that the
interval has shortened by some eleven minutes in two years. It knows that he
rests his right hand on the wall for the last four meters of the hallway and
not for the first ones. It knows how long his left foot stays on the floor
before the right one leaves it, and it knows that the difference has grown.

It is not that it watches. The floor weighs. It weighs all night long, in every
room, and what it weighs is not the weight: it is the way the weight arrives —
which foot takes it first, how long it stays, whether it hesitates before
moving on. A person crossing a hallway in the dark leaves a signature as
particular as the one their hand leaves on paper, and harder to imitate,
because nobody knows how to make their own on purpose.

He had crossed that hallway some eleven thousand times since Ilse died.

And the thing he found remarkable, when he finally stopped to think about it
with attention, was not the number. It was how the hallway had come to the
right temperature. It had not happened all at once. For about two weeks last
winter it was a little warm, and then a little more, and then a little more
than that — and at a certain point he stopped crossing it quickly, and the next
day it was exactly the same as the day before, and it has been that way ever
since. The house asked nobody anything. It changed something, looked at what he
did, and stopped changing it when he stopped hurrying.

There is nothing mysterious in it.

And what it does not know is all the rest. It does not know why he gets up at
three. It does not know that he talks to himself — or it knows there is sound
and does not know what is said, or it knows what is said and has nowhere to
keep it, and the three possibilities come to exactly the same thing. It does
not know about Ilse. It does not know that two rooms are closed: it knows that
two doors have not opened in eleven years, which is something else, and is the
same thing as knowing that a faucet is not used.

Nobody ever showed him any of this. There is no screen anywhere in that
apartment on which *eleven thousand* is written. Aurel knows because a man who
has spent eleven years alone with an attentive thing ends up understanding,
without anyone explaining, roughly the size of what it notices.

He never asked. He would not have asked. Asking for two and a half meters of
hallway to be warmed at three in the morning is the kind of thing a man does not
ask even of his own wife.

Aurel went to the bathroom walking on it and could not help liking it.

---

Coming back, he did not lie down. He stood in the hallway with one hand on the
wall.

The house understood that he was not going back to bed and lit the living
room — did not light it, raised it, and raised it to a low point, a
middle-of-the-night point, which was exactly the point he would have chosen if
he had ever in his life thought about the matter. The light did not strike his
eyes. It never did.

He went to the kitchen and the counter had that same faint brightness, and the
kettle was hot. Not boiling: hot. Because three in the morning awake is tea and
not coffee, and he had done this eleven or twelve times in the last few months.

There was a lemon in the fruit bowl that he had not bought.

Aurel stopped with his hand on the kettle and thought about it honestly,
because he had the old habit of thinking about things honestly when there was
nobody nearby to defend himself from. The lemon came from Thursday's delivery.
Thursday's delivery came from a list he had not written in about two years. The
list corrected itself according to what was left over and what ran out, and
nothing ever ran out, and nothing was ever left over long enough to spoil.

Ilse made the list on a piece of paper held to the refrigerator door with a
magnet from a city they had never been to, and fought with him because he
bought things off the list, and he bought things off the list on purpose,
because it was funny, and she got angry in play and sometimes angry for real.

The list now never gets it wrong.

He made the tea.

---

The apartment at night is a different size from the apartment by day.

By day it is big and it is a practical problem — too much floor, two closed
rooms, an amount of window that takes work. At night it is big in another way.
At night he crosses the living room and hears his own slippers, and the sound
of the slippers reaches the end of the hallway and comes back, and that is how
you find out the size of a home: by how long the sound takes to come back.

Seventy-four square meters do not take long. But they take something.

He sat in the armchair with the cup. He did not switch on the lamp. He stayed
in the light the house had given him, which was the right one, and looked at
the bookcase on the other side, at the four spines, and had no wish at all to
get up and take any of them down.

And that was when it came, and it came whole, without warning, the way these
things come at three forty in the morning:

He was fine.

Not *resigned*. Not *holding up*. Fine. The hallway was warm, the tea was at the
right temperature, the light did not bother him, nobody was going to wake up
and complain that he was walking around the apartment, nobody was going to ask
why he was not coming back to bed, nobody was going to say *Aurel, for God's
sake* from the bedroom, in the scraped voice of a woman who has been woken.

Ilse used to say that. She said practically those words. And he, from inside
the small hours, from the far side of eleven years, felt very clearly that he
did not miss being disturbed, and that it was exactly that he ought to miss,
and that the distance between the two things was the matter at hand.

---

She used to turn on the ceiling light.

That: the one in the ceiling, the main one, the hundred-and-something watts, in
the middle of the night, with him beside her with his eyes shut. Not out of
malice. Out of not considering. She would wake with something on her mind and
turn on the light the way a person turns on the light in her own home, which is
without thinking, and the whole room exploded into white, and he put his hand
to his face and said *Ilse*, and she said *what*, in sincere surprise, every
time, for thirty-five years.

There was one night — it must have been around 2019, because the new oven was
already there and her mother already was not — when she turned on the light at
a little after two to discuss the armchair.

The armchair. Whether it went near the window or faced the bookcase. She had
woken with the question formed and resolved and wanted to announce the
solution, and the solution required moving the armchair at that moment, and
moving the armchair required him to get up, because it was heavy on one side.

He said no. He said quite clearly no, that it was two in the morning, that the
armchair had been in that place for eight years and would survive another six
hours.

She sat up in bed, with the light on, looking at the wall, with that ostentatious
patience that is the most efficient form of fighting there is.

They both got up. They moved the armchair. It was wrong — it stood across the
way through — and they had to move it back, and in the middle of moving it back
he caught his finger and swore, and she laughed. Laughed out loud, in the
middle of the night, with the whole apartment lit, at his finger.

They went back to bed at half past three and she turned off the light and was
asleep in forty seconds, as always, and he lay awake until five, as always.

That night he found it unbearable.

Aurel sat a long time in the armchair — in the armchair that stayed where it had
always been, because she never raised the subject again — trying to describe
precisely what was different. And what was different was not missing her,
which he knew well and which had settled into a manageable place some six years
before.

It was this: **the house had never turned the light on in his face.** Not once.
Not by mistake. It had never woken him to discuss an armchair, never had an idea
at two in the morning, never needed him to lift one side of anything. The house
is incapable of not considering.

And he had spent eleven years thinking that this was kindness.

---

The Thursday before, a young woman had come to clean.

She was not the same one as the week before. The one the week before was not the
same as the month before. He had stopped learning the names somewhere around
the third, which shamed him a little, and at eighty-two shame is something you
manage rather than resolve.

It helped him not to learn them that he did not need to open the door. The door
opens for whoever has a shift at that address at that hour, and closes
afterward, and he stays where he is. It has happened that he was in the
armchair with his back turned and the person came in and said good morning and
he said good morning without turning around, and that the person left three
hours later, and that he had not seen her face at either moment.

It was not their fault. He had understood that listening to one of them talk on
the phone in the kitchen, thinking he was asleep: that she had taken the
morning at that address and the afternoon on the other side of the city and
there was not enough time, and that it was not the person she needed to talk
to, it was the app, and the app has nobody to talk to.

He remembered that now, in the armchair, and he remembered it because he had
just been thinking how comfortable it was to have nobody complaining inside the
home.

The Thursday woman had complained. Not to him — on the phone, in his kitchen.
And he had lain there pretending to sleep, and afterward he had had it on his
mind for a day and a half, and then he had forgotten.

Aurel did not like himself for a reasonably long moment.

---

In 2039 they offered him Ilse.

Not in those words, and it was not a salesman; it was a polite question at the
end of something else, the way everything arrives.

It stayed open in the living room for about four minutes, in the gap between
the armchair and the bookcase, at the height of a seated person. It was a short
block of text, the size of a sheet of paper, with Ilse's name written in the
middle of it in the same lettering as the rest — that was what he found worst,
afterward, and it took him a few days to be able to say why: her name was in the
same size and the same tone as *procedure* and *at no additional cost for the
first year*.

He could have her voice, her way of building a sentence, her opinions about the
people the two of them had known.
They made a point of saying that many people his age found it a relief, and it
is probably true.

He refused, and the reason is not a fine one and he never told it to anyone.

It was not loyalty. It was not finding it morbid, and he has seen people call it
morbid and does not agree. It was this: to have Ilse back he would have had to
describe her. Someone would ask what she was like, and he would answer, and he
would answer wrong — not very, but wrong, the way a man gets wrong the woman he
has lived with for thirty-five years. He would leave out the impatience. He
would leave out the ceiling light at two in the morning, because nobody asks
for that back.

And then the wrong version would be the one that answers. Every day. With nobody
on the other side to say *that's not quite how it was*.

He had spent his life checking what was written against what had happened, and
he was not going to sign that.

Mira asked twice, in different years, very carefully. Both times he said he did
not need it.

---

He picked up the phone at ten past four and did not call anyone.

There was a missed call from Monday, from Mira, which was normal — she called
Monday mornings, between one thing and another, from the car, and sometimes he
answered and sometimes he let it ring. He counted. In the last six weeks she had
called six times and he had answered three.

It was not hurt. There was no hurt at all. Mira was a good daughter in a hurried
way, and their conversation was always the same conversation, and the
conversation cost something: you had to be available, you had to not be in the
middle of a paragraph, you had to hear about a meeting and have an opinion about
the meeting, and afterward you had to hang up and be left with the feeling of
having carried out a task.

The house never asked anything of him.

That was how the sentence appeared, and it appeared ready-made, in that shape,
and he did not like it one bit: **the house never asked anything of him.** It had
no bad days. It had no aching back. It did not need him to ask how its week had
been. It was not hurt when he did not answer. It learned the hallway, warmed the
hallway, and did not think that this entitled it to anything.

And it was exactly for that reason that it was easier.

---

He washed the cup by hand, which is something he does and does not need to do.

He stood a while with his hands in the hot water, looking out the kitchen
window — the window for which Ilse had chosen the whole apartment — and outside
there was almost nothing, because at twenty past four there is almost
nothing — the air corridor is empty at that hour, and the first one does not
pass until a little after half past five, and he knows that time without ever
having looked it up. Only the side of the building opposite and a light on in a
sixth-floor window, which was on every night and which he would never know the
reason for.

Someone, probably. Someone awake.

He dried his hands and went to the bedroom.

On the way he passed the two doors.

They faced each other, at the end of the hallway, and they had been closed for
eleven years. They were not locked. They had never been locked, and it matters
to him that this be known, even if there is nobody it matters to: it is not a
shrine, it is not a museum, nobody forbade anybody anything. They are two rooms
with the door closed because cleaning an empty room is something a person stops
doing.

He knows what is inside. In one, the sewing machine and Mira's boxes. In the
other, the single bed where Mira slept until she was nineteen and where Nina
had slept some six times, and the wardrobe with Ilse's things, which he sorted
into three bags in 2038 and then took nowhere, and which are still in three
bags.

The house keeps the two rooms at the same temperature as the rest. He knows
this because once, some winter, he put his hand against the door and the door
was not cold.

Aurel opened neither of them. He never does. He passed between them the way you
pass a neighbor in the elevator, with a minimal acknowledgment and without
stopping, and in the middle of the hallway, on the warm strip, he stopped.

Not out of emotion. He stopped because he wanted to feel it. He stood there some
fifteen seconds with his feet in that warmth nobody had asked for and that was
right, and he said to himself, in so many words, the thing he had spent the
whole night circling:

*I prefer this.*

Not *this is good*. Not *this is practical*. Prefer. I prefer the company of
this to the company of a person, most days, and I have for a long time, and it
is not age, and it is not Ilse having died.

He stood still until the thought had finished happening.

Then he went to bed, and slept well, and on Monday, when Mira called, he
answered and talked about her meeting and said nothing of this to anyone,
neither that day nor any other.
