It was raining in that way that does not get you wet and gets in the way.

Voss left at five thirty-five, five minutes after his usual time, which happened
because he stopped on the ground floor to take off his badge and put it away,
which is something he always does in the same place, beside the door, against
the wall.

He put the badge in his inside pocket. He put the book under his arm so he could
get into his coat. He got into his coat.

Someone said *good night, Voss* at the revolving door, and he said good night.

---

He went down the seven steps and turned right, toward the stop for the 12, and
walked about forty meters.

And he heard a voice behind him.

"Young man."

He did not think it was for him. Nobody has called him young man in some twenty
years.

"Young man. You, sir."

---

It was a woman in her early thirties, in a gray coat, with a big bag on her
shoulder, and she had his book in her hand.

"You left it on the steps."

---

It took him a few seconds.

A paper book is a thing you can leave on top of a balustrade, and that is one of
the reasons he buys secondhand. Voss has never said so out loud because it would
sound like an opinion about the world, and he has no opinion about the world.
It is practical: what can be lost is what can be given back.

He ran his hand along his arm, on the side where the book had been, and it was
not there, and he remembered the movement with complete clarity — putting it
under his arm, getting into his coat, and at some point between the sleeve and
the shoulder resting it on the balustrade.

"I did leave it," he said.

"It was on the stone. It was going to get wet."

She held it out.

---

It was a secondhand copy, paperback, with a fold in the top corner and someone
else's name written in pen on the title page. It had cost the equivalent of a
fare and a half.

He took it.

"Thank you very much," he said. "Really. Thank you."

"Don't mention it."

---

And that was all.

She gave a small nod, turned, and walked back the way she had come, which was
the direction of Block C, and then went straight past Block C and on to the stop
on the other side.

She said nothing more. She asked nothing. She did not look back.

---

Voss stood still with the book in his hand.

Then he saw.

She had a paper in her left hand, folded in three, a full sheet, and he saw the
fold and saw the margin, and a person who has worked twenty-two years in a
building recognizes that building's paper from fifteen meters away.

And you could see, at the top, at the height of the first fold, the prefix of
the protocol number.

*G.*

---

G is allocation review.

He had done all the allocation reviews that Thursday. All of them. It is a
low-volume type — four, five a day on the whole floor — and that week they had
all landed on his desk because Larsen was on leave.

He denied four that day.

---

Voss stood on the sidewalk for a length of time he could not measure afterward,
which for him is rare.

The woman was already at the stop on the other side, her back to him, the bag on
her shoulder.

He did not go after her. He wants that to be clear to himself, and he kept
repeating it later, during the night, as if he were accounting to someone:
going after her would be worse. Going after her to say what. To ask which of the
four she was. To explain the parameter. To say that it was correct — and it was
correct, he was sure of that, he had checked each of the four against the table.

There is no sentence to say there. He looked for one and there is none.

So he went to the stop for the 12 on his own side, and the 12 came, and he got
on.

---

On the bus he opened the book and did not read.

---

The problem is not guilt. He tried guilt first, on the way, in the methodical way
he tries things, and guilt did not fit: guilt presupposes an error, and there was
no error. If one of the four denials had been wrong, he would know what to do —
he would have a procedure, he would have a form, he would have the second
instance, and he would have slept badly for a reason that has a name.

He made no error.

The problem is something else and it took him until after midnight to be able to
put it into words, and he put it into words standing in the kitchen with the
light off:

**She had nothing to gain.**

---

Voss spends the whole day, every day, inside a system in which every action has
a declarable reason. That is what a request is. That is what a claim is. The
applicant wants something and says what he wants, and the reviser checks whether
what he wants fits, and that is the whole structure of the world he works in,
and it works, and he believes in it.

She wanted nothing.

She did not know his name when she picked up the book — she picked it up because
it was on the stone and was going to get wet. And when he thanked her, she said
*don't mention it*, and walked away, and did not use the next four seconds,
which were hers, and in which she could have said anything.

He keeps thinking about those four seconds.

A woman who came out of the building with a paper folded in three, who had gone
back to the building for some reason and had not managed to settle whatever it
was because the window closes at five, had in front of her a man in a coat with
his badge in his pocket, and had in her hands a thing he wanted back.

It was her whole moment.

And she gave him the book and said don't mention it.

---

There is no field.

That is the sentence he arrived at, in the kitchen, at twenty past one in the
morning, and it is technically meaningless and he knows it is.

He has spent twenty-two years learning to read what people want. That is the
whole of his competence: to take a nine-line claim and find inside it the
subject, the fact alleged and what is being asked. It is a work of translation.
There is always a subject. He has never, in twenty-two years, received a request
without a subject, because a request without a subject is not a request.

And he was standing in the dark trying to perform, on a thing that had happened
on a sidewalk, the only operation he knows how to perform, and the operation
would not run, and he had no other.

---

He went to bed at ten past two.

He lay awake.

At half past three he got up, drank some water, and stood in the living room
looking at the bookcase — the hundred and twenty secondhand books, some with
someone else's name on the title page — and at the paperback on the table, dry,
with the fold in the top corner.

It was whole.

Nothing had gotten wet.
