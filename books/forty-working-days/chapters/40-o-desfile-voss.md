He went down at nine forty without having decided to go down.

He got up at six, made coffee, put the cup in the slot, and stayed. By nine thirty the
noise was already coming up through the window — the noise of a closed street,
which is different from an open one, duller and without cars — and at some point
he was in his coat in the corridor of the building, and then in the elevator.

He had nothing to do that Saturday. That is not a sad sentence. It is a fact
about his schedule: he has nothing to do on any Saturday, and never has had, and
has always been fine with it.

What had changed is that he also had nothing to do on Monday.

---

The fifth row is a bad position and he knew it would be bad when he chose it.

He chose it anyway, because to get farther forward you have to get past people,
and getting past people means saying excuse me, and saying excuse me in a crowd
is a negotiation — the person gives up half a step, you thank them, and someone
beside them comes off worse.

He stayed where he had arrived.

---

Sanitation went by. Transport went by. Public works went by.

He read the signs on each block out of professional habit, and noticed that the
signage was good: lettering large enough to be read at twenty meters, correct
contrast, the name of the department on top and its remit underneath in a
smaller size. Someone had thought about it.

He thought it was well done.

---

At some point he put his hand to the inside pocket of his coat.

He was not looking for anything. It is a movement — the badge goes there, and he
has kept the badge there every day for twenty-two years when leaving the
building, and the hand makes the trip by itself a few times a day without his
telling it to.

The pocket was empty, which is normal on a Saturday.

He lowered his hand.

---

There was not one person on that street who knew who he was.

He thought this clearly, without drama, the way he establishes things, and it is
not the same as feeling alone: he has been alone for many years and his
solitude is a stable and reasonably comfortable condition, like a piece of
furniture.

It is something else.

On that street there were — he estimated, out of habit, by quadrant and density,
three people per square meter where it was tight — something like twelve
hundred people in his stretch. A negligible number of them had been through
Block C. Some portion had received a document with four lines and a name in the
footer.

None of them would recognize the name if they read it, and none would recognize
the face at all, because the face never appears.

He had spent eighteen years deciding things about people who knew absolutely
nothing about him, and there, standing in the fifth row, that stopped being an
operational advantage and became simply the description of his situation.

---

At ten twenty-eight the marshals began asking the crowd to move back.

They came in pairs, along the edge, with a vest and a small loudspeaker, saying
*please, one step back, one step back, thank you*, and they came up the line.

Voss saw them from a distance, some fifteen meters before they reached him.

It was because of the next block, which was wider — you could see it at the
bend — and the roadway had to be clear up to the white line.

---

The instruction was correct.

That was the first thing he thought, and he thought it automatically, the way he
reads a classification: if the machine is wider, the lane has to be bigger; if
the lane has to be bigger, the crowd steps back; if the crowd steps back,
someone has to ask, and it is better that they ask in pairs, coming up along the
edge, fifteen meters ahead, so that the step back happens as a wave and not as a
fright.

Well designed.

He would have designed it that way.

---

They reached him.

"Please, one step back. One step back. Thank you."

And the whole fifth row took one step back.

The man in front. The lady in the cap. The couple with the boy up on their shoulders.
Voss.

One step back, at the same time, and the whole row pressed against the row
behind, and someone behind grumbled, and the woman next to him said *sorry* to
someone and someone said *don't mention it*.

---

He took the step.

There was nothing to it. He took the step back along with everyone, with both
hands at his sides, and made way.

He was not punished. He was not identified. He was not treated any differently
from any of the twelve hundred people in that stretch, and that is exactly what
he had defended for twenty-two years, in writing, against applicants who
claimed personal circumstances: the rule applies equally, and applying equally
means applying to you as well, and a man who accepts that only when it suits him
believes in nothing.

He believed.

He went on believing there, in the fifth row, one step farther back than he had
been a minute before, while the wide block came into the straight.

It was a single thing, and it filled the avenue from line to line. It moved on
wide, low tracks, and above them the body rose smooth to some four meters, with
no window and no door, sand-colored like the sanitation ones but matte. Along
both flanks there was a row of square doors, some twenty on each side, all
closed, and behind each door there was a unit — you could see the tops of the
shoulders through the gap at the top, all at the same height, motionless, and
the twenty heads turned to the front.

It took eleven seconds to pass him, and in those eleven seconds the street went
darker on one side.

Voss noticed that the clearance to the white line had come out at forty
centimeters on each side, which is the specified clearance, and thought it well
calculated.

---

The school band went by at ten thirty-six.

He heard it. It was a school marching band playing a march he did not know,
with some brass slightly off, and it went by in about forty seconds.

He had no opinion about it.

He could not, afterward, on the Monday, remember a single bar.

---

Nobody looked at him.

He stayed until the end, because leaving in the middle would have meant saying
excuse me.
