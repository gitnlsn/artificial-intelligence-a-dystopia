The first case of Monday came in at three minutes past eight.

Man, forty-one, asking that the count of his contribution time include a period
in 2029 recorded under another registration. He attached three documents. Two
were the right ones. The third was a handwritten declaration, which is not a
document.

Voss read the four lines and the claim, checked the two valid documents against
the history, saw that the one thing missing was exactly the thing the applicant
said he had no way of obtaining, and denied it.

Eight eleven.

He did not hesitate and did not think about it again.

---

The building opens at half past seven. He arrives at seven twenty and waits
outside, on the first step, because going in before the hour is something he
does not do.

His desk is the fourth in the third row, and it is the same as all the others: a
pale top a meter and twenty wide, no drawer, nothing on it. The desks on the
eighth floor are the same because they have no need to be different — the work
does not stay in them, it stays above them, in the air, and leaves with the
person. A floor of sixty empty desks, at half past seven in the morning, looks
like a warehouse of tabletops.

He twice turned down the desk by the window when it came free, once in 2041 and
once in 2045, which on that floor has become an anecdote he knows about and that
does not bother him. The anecdote does not understand the objection: at a window
desk the material is fainter against the glare and a person spends the day
tilting their head.

The cases arrive classified. That is done before him and it is done well.

An open case takes up about eighty centimeters of desk and rises about forty
above the top. The request sits in the middle, facing him. The suggested
framing sits just above the request, shorter, in slightly smaller lettering, and
it is the only part that comes in a different color — a warmer gray, which is
how the whole floor knows it was suggested and not decided. The applicable
provisions sit on the right, stacked, the most likely at the front. The
applicant's history sits on the left, already open, and already in the order in
which he will want to read it: first what contradicts the request, then the
rest.

Voss checks the suggestion in every case. When it goes against him, he drags the
suggested framing off with two fingers and it shrinks and disappears, and the
warmer gray leaves the desk, and what remains is only what he wrote. He does
this about three times a month.

He is in favor, and not out of laziness. The less that depends on who picked up
the case, the better. It is the same sentence he says about everything, and he
says it without any irony.

He has lunch in the square. Always the same. He comes back five minutes early.

He leaves at half past five. He does not stay late, never has, and considers
staying late a way of saying the day's work was badly organized.

---

He is fifty-one and he grew up in Brenna.

This is not something he tells. Not because he is ashamed — he is not — but
because telling where you came from, in the place where he works, is nearly
always a way of asking for something, and he noticed that very early and never
again wanted any part of it.

His father drove a delivery truck and then did not anymore. His mother cleaned
at three addresses, and about one of them, in Marvik, she complained of the
stairs — an old building, with no elevator that worked, where she climbed twice
a week with the bucket.

There were four children. He is the third.

---

In 2013, at seventeen, he applied for a place in technical training.

There were two places and eleven candidates, and he remembers the eleven because
he read the list pinned to the wall. One of them was the son of a man who knew
the director. Everybody knew that, and everybody said it was a waste of time,
and his mother told him to apply anyway because it cost nothing.

The place went by score.

Grades, plus length of residence, plus declared household income, weighted. The
list came out, and he was first, and the man's son was fifth, and there was no
conversation, and there was no lunch, and there was no phone call from anyone to
anyone.

The criteria beat the favor.

He was seventeen, and he understood that day, with a clarity he never lost
again, that the only thing that had ever been on his side in his whole life was
a table.

---

That was what turned everything else.

He did the technical course, then the night course, then went onto the
verification floor, then moved to review in 2038. Every step by examination, by
score, by published criteria. He never asked anyone for anything and nobody ever
needed to ask for anything on his behalf.

And he knows — knows with a precision his colleagues do not have because they
never needed to — that the thing that separated his life from his brothers' and
sister's was a rule applied coldly by a man who never knew his name.

One of his brothers died in 2033. The other is fine. His sister still lives in
Brenna.

---

The exception is the hole the favor comes back through.

That is how he puts it, and he has put it that way many times, once even out
loud, at a meeting in 2044, which he regretted, because out loud it sounds harsh
and he does not like to sound harsh.

An exception is always asked for by someone with a good reason. That is the
difficulty and that is the trap: nobody asks for an exception without a good
reason. What changes from one applicant to the next is not the quality of the
reason — it is the capacity to put the reason into words, to attach a document,
to come back, to know someone who can explain, to have a free afternoon to go to
Block C.

And those capacities are distributed exactly the way everything else is
distributed.

Open the exception and the queue stops being a queue. It becomes a surface with
soft spots, and the people who know where the soft spots are are not the people
who need it most: they are the people who have time, and vocabulary, and a
cousin.

He does not defend the criteria because the criteria are good. The criteria are
bad. He defends the criteria because they are the only thing that has ever been
on his side.

---

At half past five he closed the desk, which is passing his open hand once over
the top, from left to right. Everything gathers toward the hand and disappears,
and the pale top remains.

Seventy-one cases that Monday, of which he granted nineteen.

---

On the bus home he read.

He has read on the bus for twenty-odd years, always on paper, and it is his only
luxury and it costs almost nothing because he buys secondhand. That week he had a
nineteenth-century French novel that he had read in 2036 and had decided to read
again.

He reads slowly. He moves his lips a little when he reads and he knows it.

He got off at the usual stop, bought bread, went up.

---

His apartment has a living room, a bedroom and a balcony one meter deep. There
is no photograph on the wall. There is a bookcase with perhaps a hundred and
twenty books, all secondhand, some with another person's name on the title page,
which he likes.

He ate at half past seven. He washed the plate.

At nine forty he turned out the light.

And here is the thing that should be said about Voss, and that needs to be said
now, before anything else: he slept well.

He did not sleep with effort, did not sleep after persuading himself of
anything, did not lie awake an hour thinking about the forty-one-year-old man and
the handwritten declaration that is not a document.

He slept the way a man sleeps who has worked all day and done the work right.
