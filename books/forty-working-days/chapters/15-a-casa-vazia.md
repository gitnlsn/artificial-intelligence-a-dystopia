The door code still worked.

That was the first thing, and Rita only understood it mattered some two hours
later: she keyed in the four numbers, the bolt slid, the door opened. Nothing
had been canceled there. The building still thought she should come in.

"Mr. Vilmar?"

The kitchen was on the left and the light was off.

"Mr. Vilmar, it's Rita."

She already knew there was nobody. She knew it at the door, before she went in,
the way you know — a house with someone inside is different from a house
without, and the difference arrives before the rest. She called anyway, both
times, because it is what you do.

---

He is eighty-one and he does not take his pill.

That is the thing she tells about him, when she tells, because it is the funny
thing: a man of eighty-one who hides tablets under his plate, as brazen as you
like, like a child. She found two or three a week. He said he had taken it. She
said *and what's this?*, and he said that one must be from yesterday, and the
two of them laughed, and he took it.

That was what she had told Elias about on Sunday. *A gentleman in Brenna who
won't take his pill.* And Elias had asked his name and remembered the name the
next day.

---

The light came up when she walked in, the way it comes up. The kettle was hot,
because at seven in the morning on a Thursday somebody has coffee in that house,
and because that has been true for years.

Nobody had told the apartment either.

At two in the afternoon it would remember the pill. It would remember on Friday
too, and on Saturday. A house knows the time of the tablet the same way it knows
the time of the kettle, and knows nothing else — it knows the two o'clock one
was not taken, and had known that two or three times a week for two years, and
had never had anything to do with it, and still did not.

---

The apartment was tidy.

Not tidy like someone who has left: tidy like Thursday. The bed was made the way
he made it, with the sheet folded over the blanket, badly pulled on the wall
side because he did not go around. There was a cup in the sink with coffee in
the bottom. His radio, which has not been turned on since before Rita started
coming to that house, was in its usual place, turned toward the armchair.

Thursday's pill box was open and empty. Friday's was closed.

No chair knocked over. No door open that should have been shut. Nothing on the
floor.

And nobody.

---

She stood in the middle of the living room with the bag still on her shoulder.

Then she took out her phone.

*Shift 5512-D — canceled.*

*Logged: 07:04.*

She had got on the 12 at ten past seven.

---

Rita spent about two minutes looking for the reason.

She already knew she would not find it — she had known since Saturday, since the
night she spent fifteen minutes looking for somewhere to write —, and she looked
anyway, because a shift canceled with the patient absent is not the same thing
as a lateness warning, and some part of her still thought there ought to be a
difference.

There was not. *Canceled* is a word and the word was there, and next to it the
time, and nothing else.

She asked the helpline.

There is a helpline. People don't believe that, but there is, and it answers at
once. She asked about a canceled shift and the answer arrived before she had
finished asking: canceled shifts are communicated in the app, and no further
procedure is necessary. It was correct and it was complete.

She asked another way. The same answer came, in the same order, in the same
words.

She spent eleven minutes on it, and the eleven minutes were not spent waiting.
There is no waiting. They were spent looking for the question.

That was the problem. She did not know what she was going to ask. *I'm in his
house and he isn't.* And then the person on the other end, if there were one,
would ask what she wanted, and the honest answer was: I want to know.

There is no protocol for *I want to know*.

---

He could be in the hospital. He could be at his niece's, who lives three blocks
away and who once took him for the weekend and nobody told Rita and she arrived
and the house was empty and the next day everything was normal. He could have
fallen. He could have died at five in the morning.

He could have been reallocated.

That is the possibility she took longest to look at, because it is the silliest
and the most likely: someone may have changed his registration, or the service
address, or the modality, and the shift was canceled because the shift stopped
existing, and Mr. Vilmar is at this moment sitting in his niece's kitchen eating
bread, alive, well, knowing nothing about any of it.

If it is that, she will come back on Tuesday and he will be there.

If it is not that, she will never know, because there is nobody to tell her.

---

She washed the cup.

It was the first thing. She did not decide — she had the bag on her shoulder,
saw the cup in the sink, and washed it, and dried it, and put it in the top
cupboard, which is where he keeps it.

Then she pulled straight the side of the sheet he does not pull straight.

Then she opened the bedroom window for eighteen minutes, which is what she does,
to air the room, and stood looking down the light well while it aired.

Then she watered the plant, a thick-leaved thing he calls a dumb cane and that
is not one.

Then she checked Friday's box and Saturday's, which were right, and there was
nothing to check.

Then she wiped down the counter.

---

At eight forty she was standing in the kitchen with the cloth in her hand and
the counter already dry.

She stayed like that for a while, and it was not uncomfortable. Standing still
had never cost her anything, and that is why the thing took so long to have a
name.

And that was when she stopped.

It was not kindness. She wants to be exact, because there are people who would
have done all that out of kindness and she knows it was not so with her that
morning, and she is not going to give herself a credit that is not hers.

She did not know what to do with her body.

A person who works with the body has the whole day built on top of tasks. You go
in, you do, you leave, and while you are doing you do not have to decide
anything, and the nearest thing to rest there is in that life is the interval
between two known tasks. Strip the sheet. Put on the cream. Check the box. Make
the coffee.

Take the task away and what is left is a body standing in the middle of a
kitchen that is not its own.

So she gave herself tasks until they ran out, and they ran out at eight forty.

---

She left at eight forty-five.

She pulled the door to and tried it to see that it had locked, the way she tries
it. She went down. In the lobby there was no doorman because there has been no
doorman since 2043.

Outside, on the sidewalk, she stood still for a few seconds with her hand on the
strap, trying to remember whether she had turned off the bedroom light and
knowing perfectly well that she had.

Then she went to the stop.

---

She checked the app that night, and on Friday, and on Saturday.

His name left her list on Friday morning. Not with a notice: it simply was not
there anymore, and the list closed the gap.

She sat looking at the closed list for a while, looking for the line that would
say why, with a stubbornness she recognized and was already beginning to be
ashamed of.

The following Tuesday she had no shift in Brenna.
