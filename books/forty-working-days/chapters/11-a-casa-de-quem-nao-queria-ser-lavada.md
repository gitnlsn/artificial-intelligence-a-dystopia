Mrs. Eszter is eighty-six and has a system.

The system is this: when Rita arrives, she is sitting in the armchair in the
living room with both hands in her lap, dressed, her hair combed, her cardigan
buttoned to the top. She gets ready in order to refuse. It matters that she be
presentable at the moment she says she is not going to bathe, because a dirty
old woman asking not to be washed is one thing and a lady with her hair combed
saying thank you, not today is another.

"Good morning, Mrs. Eszter."

"Good morning, dear. Not today."

"Not today what?"

"Not today."

And Rita says *okay* and goes to put the water on to heat anyway, because that
is part of it, and both of them know it is part of it.

The house would do the bath.

It would do it at eight, at the right temperature, and the right temperature is
something the house knows about Mrs. Eszter better than Rita knows it. It is in
her tier. Three times a week, and in four years it has not missed once.

Hence the basin.

Water in a basin gets cold. It is the only water in that house that gets cold.

---

When Rita walks into a house, the house writes for her.

It appears on the inside of the door, at shoulder height, and moves with her
from room to room: the shift number, what is scheduled, what is done. Above the
boxes of medicine sits the time for each one. Above the bed sits the date the
pillowcase was last changed. And in the lower corner there is a clock that is
not the time — it is what is left of the shift — and it counts backward, and
Rita has learned not to look at it, and looks.

Mrs. Eszter has hers. It is a different thing, and Rita has never seen it: she
knows it exists because the old woman sometimes looks at a point in the air
between the armchair and the window and answers something nobody said. In the
first weeks Rita thought she was confused. Then she understood that she was
reading.

Mrs. Eszter's goes down. It goes down about twice a shift, and the old woman
taps the arm of the chair twice and waits, and sometimes she waits a good while.
Rita's has never gone down — not there, and not on the day the whole block's
went.

On the same wall of the living room, the two of them read different things.

That isn't a subject.

---

The house is a single story, at the end of a street in Brenna that is still
cobbled for the last thirty meters because the asphalt stopped there in 2019 and
never went on.

Overhead they pass all the time. They go by high, in a corridor, one every two
or three minutes in the morning, and from down here what you see is the pale
belly and the four round housings, and when one turns toward the river you can see the
whole of its tilt.

None comes down there. There is nowhere to: a landing pad needs a concrete slab,
and the houses on Mrs. Eszter's street have tile roofs. In four years Rita has
not seen one land in Brenna a single time.

You can tell the time by the interval, the way you tell the time by buses.

There is a lace curtain that she washes every two months and lets nobody touch.
There are four photographs on top of a radio that has not been switched on in
some twenty years and that came with her in 2007: a man in a suit, two boys of
about ten, and a house that is not this one.

The house in the photograph has a different roof and a tree in front. Rita has
never asked.

---

Mrs. Eszter came to this city in 2007, at forty-six, and spent forty years
speaking the language here with an accent that she knows she has and that
annoys her.

She speaks well. She speaks better than a lot of people who were born here,
with the care of someone who learned as an adult — she uses words nobody else
uses anymore, words out of books, and she builds the whole sentence before she
starts to speak. She never stumbles.

Except when she is angry.

When she is angry she switches. Not on purpose, and she does not even notice —
Rita is almost certain she does not notice — and it is not the whole sentence.
It is the numbers.

---

That Sunday it took an hour and ten minutes.

It began at eight twenty, with the water already cooling in the basin, and Rita
doing what she always does, which is not to insist. She made the bed. She
changed the pillowcase. She watered the plant in the kitchen window, which is
hers, Mrs. Eszter's, and not the house's, and which came from a cutting a
neighbor gave her in 2011.

And every so often she passed through the living room and said something that
had nothing at all to do with a bath.

That is technique. Nobody taught it. Rita found out in her second year, on her
own, that insisting closes a person and that staying nearby opens them, and that
the difference between the two is only time, and that time is exactly what
there isn't.

At nine thirty Mrs. Eszter said:

"You are going to stay there until I give in."

"No, ma'am. I'm leaving at ten thirty."

"That's just it."

And she laughed. She laughs with her mouth closed, inward.

---

What Rita understood, and it took her about four months to understand it, is
that it is not about the bath.

Mrs. Eszter has nothing else left.

She does not decide what she eats, because the delivery comes put together. She
does not decide when to sleep, because her body decides. She does not decide
who comes into her house — whoever comes in is whoever was allocated, and eleven
different people have come in this year, and Rita is the fourth to last more
than a month. She does not decide whether she wants to stay in that house,
because the house is where she is, and moving somewhere else is a thing that
takes an amount of deciding she can no longer gather.

And in the morning, once a day, Rita arrives with a basin of hot water, and at
that moment, at that particular moment, there is a question in the world whose
answer is still hers.

She is going to say no. She knows she will say yes later, most days. But there
is an interval between the two, and the interval belongs to her.

Rita decided, in the fourth month, that she would pay for that interval out of
her own pocket.

In a house in Kalden where she did four shifts last year, before it dropped
off her roster, there is no such interval.

The one there does not walk. It stays in the bathroom, fixed to the ceiling on
a rail, and comes down when it is time: a single arm, jointed in three places,
covered in the same soft material as the hands, with the shower running inside
the arm. It comes down slowly and makes less noise than the tap. Rita has seen it
come down twice and both times thought it was beautiful.

And it asks. It asks well, too: it asks, waits, says all right, and asks again
eleven minutes later. And again. It does not get hurt and it has nowhere to be
at ten thirty.

Against a thing like that, *not today* is not an answer. It is the start of a
conversation that ends only one way.

What makes Mrs. Eszter's no worth something is that Rita is in a hurry.

---

At nine fifty-five Mrs. Eszter looked at the bathroom door and said:

"It's cold already."

"I'll heat it again."

"You'll heat nothing. It's a waste."

"I'll heat it, Mrs. Eszter."

And then she got angry.

It was quick and it was over nothing — it was because Rita took her arm to help
her up before Mrs. Eszter had decided to get up, and that is a mistake Rita has
not made in a long time and made that Sunday because she was hurrying inside.

Mrs. Eszter pulled her arm away. And she said something short that Rita did not
understand. And then, looking at the basin, she counted — slowly, with her
mouth, her chin trembling a little with anger:

Three words.

Rita has heard it some thirty times in four years. She knows the sound of all
three by heart, knows where the voice rises and where it stops, could imitate it
if anyone asked, and has never known what they meant.

Everything else Mrs. Eszter says reaches Rita in Rita's language, and neither of
them does anything to make that happen, and neither of them has ever mentioned
it, the same way nobody mentions water coming out of the tap. Not the three
words. The three arrive as sound.

For a long time Rita thought it was a fault — that they were too short, or that
the anger tangled them. Then she stopped thinking anything about it, because
what a lady of eighty-six says to a basin of water is nobody's business.

Then Mrs. Eszter took a breath, straightened her cardigan, and said, in the
language here, perfectly built:

"I apologize. It was not your fault."

---

She bathed at ten past ten.

It took eighteen minutes and it went easily and she even let her hair be
washed, which happens perhaps once a week. Rita dried her with the good towel,
the one kept separate, and put on the cream, and buttoned the cardigan back up
to the top because that is how Mrs. Eszter is.

At the door, at ten thirty-seven, Rita asked.

She does not know why. She had never asked in four years.

"Mrs. Eszter, what is it you say when you're angry? The counting."

The old woman looked at her for a while.

"I count," she said.

"I know. But what?"

"One, two, three." She shrugged. "My mother made me count to ten before I
answered back. I never got past three."

Rita laughed.

And Mrs. Eszter laughed too, the inward laugh, and then said, almost without
meaning to:

"It's the only thing I don't have to translate."

---

On the 12, on the way to the second house, Rita kept thinking about that.

Not about the sentence, which was lovely and which she would forget in two
days. About the fact that she had walked into that house for four years, three
times a week, some six hundred times, and that it had taken six hundred times to
ask a question of six words.

It had not been for lack of wanting. It was that asking takes seven minutes, and
seven minutes is what separates arriving on time from arriving late.
