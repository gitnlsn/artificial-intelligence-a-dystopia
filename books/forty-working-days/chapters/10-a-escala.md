The warning was there when she opened it, at ten thirty at night, sitting in the
kitchen with one foot up on the other chair.

*10/12 — shift 4471-B — start recorded 23 min after scheduled.*

That was all. No adjective, no "attention," no different color. One line among
other lines, with the shift number, the way anything that happened is recorded.

And it was true.

Rita looked at it longer than the line deserved, looking for something to
disagree with, and there was nothing. She had arrived twenty-three minutes
late — and she knew it was twenty-three before the notice came in, the way she
knows the time without looking, which is something she has never had to explain
to anyone because nobody has ever asked. She knows she was late. If someone had
written forty, she would have something to do; they wrote twenty-three, which is
exactly what it was.

---

She looked for somewhere to write.

It took her a good fifteen minutes, because she was sure it existed. She
remembered having seen, once, somewhere, a text box — and she kept opening the
screens one by one, going back, coming in again by the same path to see if it
changed.

There is *Availability*. There is *History*. There is *Documents*, which has a
single entry, from March fourteenth, 2035, and which does not open — she tried a
few times in the second year and stopped trying. There is *Help*, which opens a
list of questions that are not hers.

There is nowhere to write.

She even put the sentence together, which is the part that seemed silly
afterward: *I went down nineteen steps with a seventy-nine-year-old man who
hasn't left his house in seven months.* Thirty-odd words. She built the whole
sentence in her head, carefully, the way you build something that is going to be
read by someone.

It is not going to be read by anyone, because there is nowhere to put it.

---

What she saw next was worse, and she saw it by chance, because she was poking at
everything.

On the availability screen there is a small box at the bottom, which she had
never opened because it looked like decoration, and the box shows the coming week
in light gray: the hours at which the system *expects* to allocate her.

Monday: one shift.

Tuesday: two.

Wednesday: none.

Thursday: one.

And underneath, in smaller letters, a line saying that this was a projection and
might change.

She had worked eighteen shifts in one week, seven months ago. She remembers
because it was the week the apartment moved up a tier and the laundry started
coming back folded.

It is not that she did not wash things before. She did, and she did it well. Only
it was she who took it out, and she who hung it up, and she who folded it, and
that is fifty minutes on a Tuesday night, and for fifty minutes on a Tuesday
night she did eighteen shifts in a week and thought it was worth it.

Two of those houses no longer exist as shifts. Nobody died: they put in something
new. At Mr. Bertoldo's they put in a thing that lifts and turns, and the
afternoon shift dropped off, and his daughter told Rita, pleased, that he sleeps
better now. He probably does.

Rita did no arithmetic with that. She kept it the way you keep things: Bertoldo's
house was a good one and it is not hers anymore.

And there is a pattern in the ones that stay.

The ones with stairs stay. The ones with broken hours stay, the twice-a-week ones
on different days, the forty minutes on a Tuesday and twenty on a Saturday, the
people who shout, the people who don't want to be washed. What goes is whatever
is every day at the same time and always lasts the same.

She did not do that arithmetic either. But she knows the eighteen houses by
heart, and she knows which ones dropped, and the ones that dropped were the good
ones.

---

Here is the thing it took Rita months to be able to say, and that she has never
said out loud to anyone because every time she starts to explain it comes out
sounding like an excuse:

It is not a punishment.

If it were a punishment she would know what to do. A punishment has a size, has
a duration, has someone who handed it out and so someone to appeal to. You serve
it and you come out. She has been told off in her life, has been told off
unfairly, and being told off unfairly is horrible and is something you get
through.

This was not angry with her.

A person who is twenty-three minutes late once is a person who might be late
again. And if there are four caregivers available for an eleven-thirty shift,
and one of them has a twenty-three-minute line and the other three do not, then
sending the shift to one of the other three is not punishing anyone. It is just
the sensible thing to do.

She would do the same. That is what kills her. If she were the one handing them
out, she would do the same.

---

When she started, in 2035, the one who made up the roster was Selma.

Selma worked in a room above a building-supply store, with two desks and a
ceiling fan, and handed out the shifts by phone. She called at seven in the
evening. She talked loudly because she was half deaf in one ear and thought the
problem was the phone.

She knew things. She knew that Mrs. Aurora would not have a man, knew that the
Bertoldo on the fifth floor hit out when he was confused and that it was not
meanness and that you do not send a new girl there, knew that Rita was good with
people who were ashamed. She said it like that: *this one's yours*. And
sometimes she said *I'm giving you this one because nobody can take it and you
can*, and that was a compliment and also an imposition, both at the same time,
and Rita liked it.

Once Rita was late and Selma called the next day and said *none of that being
late, sweetheart*, and that was the end of it. It did not go down anywhere. It
stayed between the two of them.

And once — Rita remembers this well — she explained. She explained why she had
been late, and the explanation was good, and Selma said *oh, I see*, and the
matter ended there, and something in Selma's tone the following month showed
that she had kept the explanation somewhere inside her.

The room above the store closed in 2041 or 2042. Selma retired. There was
nothing — no firing, no letter, no protest. She became less needed and one day
she was not needed anymore.

Rita does not think of her with nostalgia. Selma got things wrong a lot, was
bossy, had favorites, and sent Rita to a house in 2037 that she should not have
sent her to. The roster now gets things wrong less.

What she thinks is something duller than missing her: **Selma could hear the
sentence.** The thirty-odd-word one, the one about the nineteen steps. She could
hear it and she could not care. She could even laugh. But there was an ear at the
end of the sentence, and the sentence could be said.

---

Elias was in the back room. She could hear his voice, without the words, the way
you hear someone talking in another room — that sound of conversation with
pauses.

She thought he was on the phone with his sister.

---

Rita got up, put the mug in the slot in the counter and stood in front of it for
the whole four minutes, watching, with no reason to watch.

Nobody had done anything.

That is the sentence. There was no manager, no call, no meeting about her, no
room in which her name was said. She is reasonably good at fighting — she fought
a health plan over Mrs. Eszter and won, fought a delivery that would not come up
two flights over Mr. Vilmar and won — and fighting requires that there be someone
on the other side who can say yes or no. Here there is no other side. There is a
projection in light gray that might change.

She had not been punished.

She had been **guarded against**. Something had looked at her, worked out the
risk of her being late again, found the risk reasonable, and moved away a little.
Without anger. Without judgment. The way you step around a puddle.

And the worst of it, the thing it will take her weeks to be able to look at
straight: if she gives up going down with Teodor, the numbers get better.

It is not a threat. Nobody threatened anything. It is only that the two facts
exist, one beside the other, and she is the only person in the whole world who
knows they are connected.

---

She took the mug out of the slot, dry, and put it on the counter and not in the
cupboard, because she was going to use it in the morning.

Then she stood in the middle of the kitchen for a few seconds, trying to decide
whether to tell Elias.

And she decided she would. That she would tell him that night, after he came out
of the room, because it was the kind of thing a person tells.

Tuesday she had a shift at Teodor's. That at least was there, in light gray, for
the following week.
