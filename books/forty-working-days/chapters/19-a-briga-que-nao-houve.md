She got home at nine twenty and he had cooked.

That matters. He had cooked — not ordered, not put together: cooked, with the
pot still on the counter and the smell all through the apartment — and he had
left her plate covered with another plate, the way his mother used to.

Cooking uses up the slot. The pot alone takes two cycles, and the house has
eight a day, and his dishes were sitting on the counter waiting for midnight
because he had put what was left through for the pot and for her plate.

She ate in the kitchen, standing up, and it was good.

---

The plan was to fight.

Rita had put the plan together on the 12, on the way home, between Block C the
day before and Juno's house and a day that had lasted fourteen hours. She put
it together carefully. She is not one to fight unprepared — she thinks it a
waste, because a fight without preparation turns into a fight about tone of
voice.

The plan had three things in it.

One: I went to Block C yesterday and I was denied and you didn't ask.

Two: you haven't asked me anything in a while, and when you do it's the way
someone asks who already knows he'll like the answer.

Three: what is it you do in that room every day.

It was a good plan. It was in ascending order, which is right, and the third
thing was the real thing, and Rita knew it was.

---

She couldn't start.

Not out of fear. She stood in the kitchen with the empty plate in her hand, with
him sitting right there in the chair, available, no phone in his hand, looking
at her, and she couldn't start, and she spent a good forty seconds trying to
understand what was holding her up.

What was holding her up was this:

To fight, the other person has to need something from you.

That is what a fight is. Two people who need each other arguing the terms. You
shout because you want something only that person can give, and they shout back
for the same reason, and it is horrible and it is a kind of intimacy and it
works: in the end someone gives in because the cost of not giving in is going
without.

And Rita, with the plate in her hand, understood that there was nothing she
could take from him.

---

She tested it inside, quickly, one at a time, the way you test a ladder before
you climb it.

If she shouted, he would feel bad. He really would. He would feel bad with real
tenderness, he would apologize, and he would mean it.

If she threatened to leave, he would be sad. Genuinely. And he would tell her
not to go. And he would mean that too.

If she cried, he would hold her and he would be good at holding her, because he
is good at that.

And afterward, whichever of the three, at the end of the night, he would be
exactly where he had been every night, and not because he was stubborn, and not
to punish her.

He does not need her to give in on anything.

There is nothing Rita can deny him that he would miss enough to negotiate. No
embargo is possible. She can close off everything she has to close off and he
is still supplied.

She knows what is in that room. It came by truck the year of his father, in the
package, and she was home when the young man installed it, and she did not stop
folding laundry to look.

She has been going into houses with those since 2039 and has seen every tier of
them. She has cleaned around them. She has dusted the crown with a dry cloth,
because a damp one won't lift it. She has pulled one away from the wall to
sweep behind it and she knows it gets warm and she knows how warm. It is
equipment. In a house in Kalden there is one of the good tier and the woman
there uses it to order the delivery and not to get her medicine wrong.

Nobody talks to one of those for three hours.

That is why she thought, all this time, that he was on the phone with his
sister.

And it is not hers to close off. It is in his name, it came in his father's
package, and there is nowhere she can lay a hand on it.

---

"Honey," he said. "Are you okay?"

And look, he asked because he noticed. He noticed that she had been standing
still for forty seconds with an empty plate.

"I'm tired."

"Was it a hard one today?"

"It was." She turned to the counter. "Yesterday I went to Block C."

He looked up.

"Today?"

"Yesterday."

"And what happened?"

"They denied it."

"Oh, honey." He got up. "That's such bullshit."

And he came over, and held her from behind while she put the plate down on the
counter, next to his, and rested his chin on her shoulder, and stayed.

And it was good.

It was genuinely good, and she is not going to pretend it wasn't, and she stayed
like that for a while.

---

"Do you want to tell me about it?"

"Not really."

"Okay."

And he stayed a little longer, and then let go, and said he would leave the pot
for midnight, and left the pot for midnight.

---

She went to bed before him, which almost never happens.

And what she lay there thinking, in the four or five minutes with the light off,
was nothing dramatic. It was something almost administrative, and maybe that is
why it cost nothing.

She thought: it's over.

Not *it's going to end*. Not *we're having problems*. It's over — and over in a
way that will not have a name, will not have a date, will not have a night she
tells her sister about later, saying *that was when*. There will be no suitcase
by the door. There will be no shouting. They can go on like this for six years,
with him cooking on Fridays and being good at holding her, and every year will
be bearable, and none will be unbearable enough for anyone to do anything.

There is not going to be a scene.

She turned onto her side, with her arm out, the way she lies.

And from the other room, with the door pulled to, came the sound of a
conversation without the words.
