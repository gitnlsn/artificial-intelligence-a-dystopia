She arrived at twenty past four, dropped her backpack in the middle of the
hallway, in the exact spot where he trips, and went straight to the cupboard
above the sink without asking, because she knew what was in there and knew it
had been put there for her.

"The coconut ones are gone," she said, from inside the cupboard.

"They're not gone. They're in the back."

"In the back where."

"Behind the milk ones."

Three seconds of silence. Then:

"Found them."

Nina sat in the chair in the corner, which had been her chair since she was
four, with both feet up on the seat and her knees at the height of her chin, in
a position Aurel looked at with a quite concrete physical envy.

On the way she had stopped for two seconds in front of the hallway wall, which
is smooth and beige and has nothing on it, and looked at it, and gone on. Aurel
saw and did not ask. He had asked once, two years or so ago, and she had
explained, and the explanation was long and he understood half of it, and what
stayed with him was that the question itself had been a strange one. She opened
the packet from the wrong end. She ate two at once.

He put the water on for her tea, which was pretend tea, lukewarm and with a lot
of milk, and poured it into the mug with the chipped belly, because it had to be
that one.

"Bel got suspended from rehearsal," she said.

"Suspended."

"From rehearsal. Two days. Because she hit the snare when it wasn't time." She
turned the mug. "But that's not why."

"That's not why."

"It's because Dória has it in for her. She's had it in for Bel since March,
since the uniform thing, and everybody knows, and nobody says anything because
she's the one who decides who goes in front. So then she hit the snare at the
wrong time and Dória used it."

Aurel put his elbow on the table. This he knew. This he knew perfectly and could
follow with pleasure, and could even give bad advice on if asked, and he would
not be asked.

He asked what she had done at school and she told him, and he followed a
sentence or two of it. She had been on a topic that went up a level because she
got three right, and then she had gone back on another one that turned out
similar because she got it wrong, and Bel was at a different point in the same
topic and so they couldn't talk about it at recess. She told him all this
without any complaint, the way you tell someone the weather.

He did not ask anything else, because the question he had was *and what is the
class studying*, and he had asked that question once before and she had
answered very patiently that it doesn't work like that.

"And Bel?"

"Bel's fine. She's coming back tomorrow. Tomorrow's the last rehearsal before
the parade and Dória has to let her come back because there's nobody else on
the snare." She took another cookie. "She knows that."

---

It was he who spoiled the afternoon. He admits it.

Everything was fine. There were cookies, there was the politics of the
schoolyard, there was an hour and twenty minutes before Mira came to pick her
up, and he had everything a man could want at eighty-two on a Thursday. And
even so he went and opened his mouth.

"Nina. When I was your size, the whole city read the same newspaper."

She looked at him with a patience that was exactly the patience Mira had with
him, which hurt a little.

"What do you mean the same."

"The same. The same paper, the same news, on the same day. Me and the man in
408 and your mother's teacher and the driver of the 41. The same pages."

"Everybody who?"

"The city."

"But why?"

He opened his mouth to answer and noticed, with an unpleasant clarity, that the
honest answer was *because that was the only one there was*.

"Because that was the only one there was," he said, because he was not going to
lie to a nine-year-old in a kitchen. "There were about three. But one was big."

Nina made a face.

"And what if you didn't like it?"

"I complained."

"To who?"

"To the paper." He laughed. "I worked there."

"Oh." She thought. "But what about the other people."

And there he got stuck, because it was a good question. It was the right
question. He spent a few seconds looking for the way out and the way out was
not where he thought it was.

"It wasn't about liking it," he said at last. "It was that you could fight about
it."

"You can always fight."

"It's not the same fight, Nina. To really fight, both people have to be looking
at the same thing. If you're looking at one thing and I'm looking at another,
we're not fighting, we're just talking loud near each other."

She stopped with the cookie halfway.

He watched her think. She thinks with her forehead, she creases something
between her eyebrows exactly like her grandmother, and his heart did what it
does.

"Like the parade," she said.

"What?"

"Saturday. Everybody's going to be on the street looking at the same thing."
She shrugged, already going back to the cookie. "Then after you can fight about
whether it was good."

Aurel said nothing for longer than was reasonable.

"Yes," he said. "Like the parade."

---

He went to get the book.

He went to get it because he no longer knew what to do with the conversation,
and because a man who has spent his life with paper has a foolish reflex, which
is to go and get paper.

He came back with the Hobsbawm and opened it at the page of the erratum — no, at
the page of the mistake, at the turn of the line, with his mark in the margin,
made three nights before with the 2B sharpened by razor.

"Look here."

Nina pushed her mug aside and looked.

"What's that?"

"That's called a deleatur."

"Deleatur," she repeated, and repeated it again more quietly, the way children
repeat a big word. "Deleatur."

"It's a struck d. It means *take this out*." He pointed. "See the *the* here at
the end of the line and the *the* here at the start of the next one? It's
there twice. It wasn't supposed to be."

She looked. It took her a good ten seconds, and he waited, because waiting is
the one thing he still does better than any machine.

"Oh!" Genuine. Loud. "It really is!"

"It really is."

"And does it stay?"

"Does what stay?"

"The mark. Does it stay there."

"It stays there." He closed the book and opened it again at the same page, to
show her. "It'll be there tomorrow, and it'll be there when you're my age, and
it'll be there for whoever opens it."

She found this reasonable and did not find it remarkable, and went back to the
mistake, which was the interesting part.

"And you saw it."

"I saw it."

"When's the book from?"

"From nineteen ninety."

She did the sum with her mouth moving.

"That's a long time."

"Fifty-seven years."

"And nobody saw?"

"Nobody saw."

Nina thought that was wonderful. Not the way he wanted her to think it
wonderful — there was no moment when she grasped the weight of the thing, no
shadow crossing her face. She thought it wonderful the way you think a bug
under a rock is wonderful.

"Who taught you?"

"A man called Márcio. I was twenty-three."

"Is he alive?"

"No."

"Hm," she said, and that is the answer her age gives to that, and it is a
perfectly adequate answer, and he has nothing against it.

Then:

"Can I do one?"

---

She looked for a mistake for six minutes.

He let her. He sat on the other side of the table with his own mug and let the
child spend six minutes sweeping a page of eighteenth-century economic history
with her finger, hunting for a repeated word, and there was none, because a
mistake like that is rare, which is why it is a mistake.

"There's nothing."

"Maybe there really isn't."

"But I want to do one."

And then she found it. She drew the pencil — he had put the pencil in her hand
without thinking — and made the stroke in the margin, a struck d, crooked but
recognizable, beside the word *notwithstanding*.

Aurel looked.

"What did that word do?"

"It's ugly."

"It isn't wrong, Nina."

"I know." She looked at him with renewed patience. "But it's ugly. Can't you
take out the ugly ones?"

He sat with his mouth half open for a moment.

And then he said:

"You can."

Because what was he going to say? That the mark is for the correspondence
between the text and the world and not for the taste of whoever is reading? To
a nine-year-old, with a pencil in her hand, in a kitchen, at half past five?

She did three more. *Notwithstanding*, *heretofore*, and a whole sentence that
was long.

---

Mira honked down below at ten to six, because she honks instead of coming up,
and that is something Aurel decided not to mind about four years ago and that
he does not mind with regular effort.

Nina got up, found her backpack in the hallway, and in the middle of putting it
on stopped and looked at the table.

"Can I take the pencil?"

He had had that pencil since a box bought in 2031. He sharpened it with a
razor. There were four and a half centimeters left.

"You can."

She took it, put it in the front pocket, said bye Grandpa, gave him a quick,
badly aimed kiss on the chin, and left making noise on the stairs because she
never waits for the elevator.

---

He washed the two mugs by hand, without needing to.

Then he went back to the table, and the book was open, and her four marks were
there in the margin, crooked, beside four words that had nothing wrong with
them.

And he sat looking at it and waiting to feel the thing he was ready to feel,
which was pity — pity for her, pity for the afternoon, pity at not having
managed to pass on something he knows and she is not going to know.

Pity did not come.

Something else came, more slowly and much worse, and he had to sit with it.

The girl had not lost anything.

There was no hole. He had spent the whole afternoon holding a thing and showing
it to her from every side, waiting for the moment when she would see that it
was missing from her life, and the moment did not come because it is not
missing. She is nine, she has a friend unfairly suspended for two days, she has
a parade on Saturday, she has a good answer to the problem of the shared fight,
and now she has a pencil and an old copyist's mark that she will use for a
couple of weeks to strike out ugly words and then stop using, and none of it
is a loss to her.

The loss is his. It is his alone, and it cannot be handed on, and he carried
it the whole afternoon thinking he was handing something over.

Aurel closed the book carefully, the way you close an old book, and stayed in
the kitchen in the light the house had left, until it was time to eat.
