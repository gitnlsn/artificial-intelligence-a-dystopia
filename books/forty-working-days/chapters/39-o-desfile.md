First came the city banner, which is boring, and then a band of grown-ups, which
was good and was not hers.

Nina clapped to be polite.

---

Then came cleaning.

She knows the cleaning ones. They go down her street before dawn, dirty, one at
a time. She had never seen them like this.

They are low, as high as her knee, wide as a door lying down, the color of
sand. They have no wheels: underneath there is a skirt of thick rubber that
nearly touches the ground. They were clean in a way she had never seen one be,
without a mark.

The avenue had been washed before dawn. They swept it anyway.

They make a sound like blowing, and fourteen together make one blow. They go
along with the same space between one and the next — the same space the whole
time, and that is what makes it look nice.

A man behind her said *look at that spacing* to another man, and Nina thought
he was right.

---

Transport was big and loud: three line cars, the long kind, no cab at the
front, with the glass going all the way down to the ground at the end, so that
you could see the empty seats from the first one to the last.

Works was bigger. A yellow thing the size of half a house, with an arm folded
three times over its body and tied with red tape so it would not open on the
way. The arm ended in a scoop turned upward, full of nothing.

And Nina started to get hungry.

---

The four-legged one was not the size of a horse.

Lu had exaggerated, obviously. It was the size of a big dog, and it walked the
way a dog walks when it is in a hurry but not running.

The legs are thin and bend backward, of a dark metal that does not shine. The
body is a short box the color of lead, and the body **does not sway**: the legs
go up and down and the box stays at exactly the same height the whole time.
From far off it looks like a dog. Up close it does not look like anything.

It makes no sound of paws. It makes a short click at each step, four clicks at
a time, always in the same order.

Three went by, and nobody walked behind them.

The whole street made a noise. It was not clapping: it was a long *ah*, a
thousand people at once, and Nina made it too without having decided to, and
then laughed because she had made it too.

---

Then came a block with the name of home assistance on a panel, and behind the
panel the units, about twelve, in two rows.

They went with the lower arm bent out in front, in the position in which you
offer your forearm for someone to hold, and they all had the arm at the same
height.

Nina found that block boring and kept looking at the bend, to see what was
coming.

---

At ten twenty-eight the marshals began asking the crowd to move back.

They came in pairs, along the edge, in vests with a small loudspeaker, saying
*please, one step back, one step back, thank you*.

Nina climbed down from the gap in the barrier and took the step, because it is
what you do. The row behind pressed together, and someone said *sorry*, and
someone said *don't mention it*.

---

And then the street went darker on one side.

It was one single thing and it was taller than the second floor, and it took up
the whole street from one side to the other, and it had no windows at all.

In the middle of it there was a row of square doors, closed. Nina counted the
ones on one side while it went by and got nineteen, and she knows she got it
wrong, because she started counting after the first one.

Behind each door there was a unit. You could see the tops of the shoulders
through the gap at the top, all at the same height, and the heads turned to
the front.

The barrier went cold under her hand the way it goes cold when a shadow passes,
and then warmed up again.

It took eleven seconds.

It was the only thing in the whole parade when the street made no noise at all.

---

Then came a part that was just cars with people inside waving, and it was
boring.

---

Then Nina saw the blue.

Her school's uniform is blue with a white stripe on the shoulder, and she saw
the white stripe way up there, still far off, before she could see anybody's
face, and she climbed back up into the gap in the barrier with both hands
squeezing the iron.

"Grandpa. Grandpa, it's my school."

They came in eight rows. Nina looked for the second row, on the left side, in
the middle, and Bel was there — in uniform, with the cap, with the snare in
front of her body hooked to the belt, and with the most serious face Nina has
ever seen in her life.

---

And then they came in.

It was the bass drum. It gave three beats alone, boom, boom, boom, and on the
fourth everything came in at the same time.

At the same time.

Nina got a fright in her body, a real fright, because for six weeks she had sat
on bleachers two steps high listening to that not happen, and it happened, in
the street, with the street full.

She screamed.

She screamed *BEEEEL* with all her strength, and Bel obviously could not look
and did not look, but the glockenspiel girl looked and Nina did not care one
bit.

---

It was out of tune.

Nina heard it. There was something in the middle of the brass that was wrong
and a trombone that was still late, the same one as always, the one at the end.

It did not matter at all.

---

It went by in about forty seconds.

Bel went by in front of her and did not look, with the serious face, with the
sticks going up and down, and on the back of her uniform there was the number
ten. And Nina stood watching them go until they turned at the pharmacy corner,
with the sound getting smaller and still together.

"Did you see?" she said. "They came in together."

"I saw, Nina."

"And then Dória told them to look at the bass drum instead of looking at her."

Grandpa was quiet for a little second.

"That's a good method," he said.

"It's the best method."

---

At twenty minutes Aurel noticed that nothing had happened.

He waited a while longer, because twenty minutes is twenty minutes and his left
leg had not been wrong in fifteen years. At twenty-five it was still not
tingling. It was something else, higher, at the thigh, that had started the
night before and that did not bother him and did not go away. They had said it
might be unpleasant at first, and they had said it with that word.

He could not call it improvement. He could not call it worse.

He shifted his weight to the right out of habit, and then shifted it back,
because he did not need to.

---

The stone of the curb was cold through his shoe.

He noticed it with a precision that amused him: cold, hard, tilted a little
toward the gutter, so that the left foot sat lower than the right the whole
time, which is the exact combination no one would choose for a man of
eighty-two to stand on for three hours.

No one had chosen it. It was a sidewalk.

For eleven years nothing in Aurel's life had been uncomfortable by accident.

---

The parade was what a parade is, and he already knew it: the inventory, with
music, going by slowly so that the people on the sidewalk could see what exists
and see that it is in order.

Cleaning went by, transport went by, works went by.

A block went by with the name of home assistance on a panel, and he read the
panel the way one reads a panel. Behind it came the units, with the arm offered
at the height at which one offers an arm, all at the same height, and none of
them was holding anyone.

Aurel watched it until it had gone and thought nothing he could put into words.

---

Then the marshals asked everyone back, and he took the step with everyone, and
the wide thing came into the straight.

He saw the shoulders through the gap at the top of the doors.

Twenty on one side, all at the same height, still, facing the front, and the
street went dark on that side for as long as the thing took to go by.

Aurel has climbed a staircase and stood in front of a man sitting at a desk, who
had a name, and who was obliged to answer that same day.

He said nothing. He kept both hands on top of the barrier until it was light
again.

---

And then her school's band came, and Aurel heard it before he saw it.

---

It was out of tune.

It was the first thing he noticed, before the blue uniform, before looking for
the girl with the snare, before Nina started shaking the barrier: there was
something in the middle of the brass that was a quarter tone off, and a
trombone coming in late with a regularity you could predict.

He went completely still.

---

A machine does not get it wrong like that.

A machine can get things wrong — it does all the time, it gets things wrong at
scale — but it does not get it wrong *a quarter tone sharp on a wind instrument
on the third note of the bar*. That mistake belongs to a fifteen-year-old boy
with a tired lip, who knows it is coming out high and cannot fix it halfway,
and who is going to keep playing because stopping is worse.

Aurel stood on the cold stone with his hand on top of the barrier listening to
thirty-one people make, with their bodies, a sound that nobody had adjusted.

He found the place in two bars, the way you find it. His right hand made a
small movement on the iron and stopped.

After that it did nothing more.

---

He tried to remember the last time.

Not the last time he heard music — he hears music every day, the house puts on
music, and the house's music is always good, always exactly good, and always at
the volume he would have wanted.

The last time he heard a sound that nobody had adjusted for him.

He could not find it. He kept looking for the whole forty seconds the band was
passing in front of him and did not find it, and he got to 2036, which was when
they bought the system, and concluded that that was probably it, and the sum
came to eleven years, and it did not seem absurd to him.

---

Nina screamed her friend's name, with the whole strength of nine years, on his
right side, so close that he felt it in his ear.

And there, standing, with the leg silent and the left foot lower than the
right, Aurel understood why he had not been able to name it.

The street was missing.

He had spent a whole afternoon looking for a word inside an apartment of
seventy-four square meters, alone, with four spines on the shelf. And there was
nothing written over anything on that avenue — he had seen it from the window
the day before, the whole stretch set up and clean, and had found it strange
without knowing why.

A nine-year-old girl had said it to him in a kitchen, with a cookie in her hand,
in three words.

He still does not have the name. He will not have it. He is eighty-two and the
name will be given by someone else, later, from far off, the way it always was.

But he knew, on the cold stone, why he had not found it. And that is different
from knowing nothing.

---

She explained the method, with a lot of emphasis, and the method was good, and he
said it was good.

He said nothing else. He did not tell Mira, four meters behind, where he had
been the day before. He did not say it was out of tune.

She had not lost anything. She still had not lost anything.

He shifted his weight onto the left leg, which said nothing, and stood looking at
the pharmacy corner, where the blue had disappeared, until he stopped hearing
it.

---

Rita stayed in the sixth row, which is where you can stand when you get there at
ten.

She could see almost nothing. She saw between one shoulder and another, in
pieces, and when something big went by she could see the top of it. A yellow
works thing went by with an arm folded over it and she saw the whole arm.

She had the bag on her shoulder. She had forgotten to take it off.

---

A block went by with a panel that said home assistance.

She saw the panel and saw what came behind it in pieces, between the heads, and
could not see it properly, and made no effort to see it any more clearly.

She kept looking at the back of the neck of the man in front of her until that
block was over.

---

She nearly left at twenty minutes.

She had a little wooden box in the bag and a whole Friday on the floor by the
counter, and she had been standing for an hour and ten minutes seeing almost
nothing.

She turned to go and there were people behind her as far as she could see.

So she stayed.

The first part of the decision to stay was not being able to leave.

---

The marshals asked for one step back and the sixth row took the step, and she
with it.

And then came the one thing that morning she had no way of not seeing.

It went over all the heads that had hidden the rest. On the flanks there were
doors, and behind each door a unit, and you could see the tops of the shoulders
through the gap at the top.

All at the same height. All facing the front.

The street went darker on one side, and nobody said anything, and it passed.

Rita moved the bag to the other shoulder.

---

Then came the school band.

---

She heard the bass drum first. Three beats alone and on the fourth everything
came in.

And the sound reached her in a way she felt in her chest before she heard it —
because she was close, because a marching band in the street is not something
you listen to, it is something that happens in the body of whoever is nearby.

Rita stood still.

She did not know what it was playing and was not going to know, and that did
not matter in the least.

Eight days before, she had stood an hour and twenty minutes on a sidewalk
because of a song that fell from a high window. On a Thursday night, in the
kitchen, she had said a name out loud and the music had come at the right
volume.

This one could not be taken out, and could not be turned down, and was not for
her.

---

It was out of tune.

That she heard and did not think bad. She thought it, as it happens,
specifically good, and did not know why.

---

At forty seconds it had gone by, and it went on getting smaller toward the
corner, and she went on hearing it a little longer, and then she could not
anymore.

---

Keeping a good thing is work. It comes up during the day, you put it aside, you
sort the order, you decide where you are going to start, and you do it all day
without calling it doing.

Rita had been doing that work for eleven years.

She noticed she had not started.

She had not set anything aside. She had not thought once, the whole morning,
about how she would tell it — not where she would start, not whether she would
start with the bass drum or with how many people there were.

It was not hurt. He had not done anything. He was going to ask how it had been,
that night, and he was going to ask properly, and he was going to listen.

She just had not started keeping, and could not say since when.

---

She stayed until the end, her shoulder aching on the side of the bag.

Being in a place with a lot of people was something she had not done in a long
time, and she could not remember the last time.

It was not sadness. It was closer to sitting down. She had been standing for an
hour and a half and even so the nearest thing she found to describe it was
sitting down.
