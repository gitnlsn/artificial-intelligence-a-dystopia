Teodor hides his left hand under the table when she arrives.

He does it every time, he has done it for fourteen months, and in the first
weeks Rita pretended not to see. Then she began to see and to say nothing,
which is something else. He knows she sees. She knows he knows. And even so,
every morning, when the key turns, the hand goes under the tabletop.

"Good morning, Mr. Teodor."

"Good morning, my dear."

He calls everyone my dear, including the boy from the pharmacy, which is a joke
the two of them have already made and which still has some life in it.

---

The apartment has two bedrooms and smells of wood, which is something Rita has
never managed to explain properly to anyone, because no wood has been cut there
since 2039. It smells of it anyway. She thinks it is in the walls.

He was a cabinetmaker for forty-one years.

The kitchen table is his. The two chairs are his — and they are different from
each other, and he explained once that the second was made nine years after the
first, that he knew more by then, and that you can see it, and he showed her
where you could see it and Rita could not see it and said she had.

The bookcase in the living room is his. The nightstand is his. The sewing box
that was his wife's is his, with a sliding lid that slides to this day without
catching.

And the left hand does not open more than about two thirds.

It opened a third when he came home. It reached two thirds in fourteen weeks,
stopped at two thirds, and for eleven months it has stayed at two thirds.

There were three applications. Rita saw the last one, because it fell on one of
her shifts: a young woman came with an insulated bag, stayed eighteen minutes,
and what she put in went into the crook of his arm and not into the hand, and
two weeks later the hand opened a little more. It does not hurt. He said it does
not hurt, and he would say if it did.

Three were authorized. It is on paper: three.

And there is continuing. Rita knows there is continuing because in a house in
Kalden, last year, a gentleman of eighty-two had it done every six weeks and his
hand opened all the way, and he complained about the taste it left in his mouth
afterward.

Teodor had three.

---

Her shift there is three hours, from eight to eleven, three times a week. It is
on the roster as *home support — hygiene, medication, assisted mobility*, which
is three words for about forty things.

His house does the rest, and does it well. It turns on before he reaches the
switch, warms the water before he asks, reminds him of the two o'clock pills
with a discretion Rita once thought was beautiful. It is ten years old and still
better than anything Rita has at home.

It does not hold anyone by the arm. It does not go down stairs. There is nothing
in it that can go down nineteen steps with a man of seventy-nine and stand next
to him on the concrete bench while the bread cart goes by. That is still done by
a person, and the person has a three-hour shift, and the next shift starts on
the other side of the district.

In the building across the street, it goes down.

Rita has seen it twice, from the sidewalk, waiting with an old woman for the car
to arrive.

It is shorter than a person, level with her shoulder, and it walks on two legs
that bend backward like a bird's, which makes the step look softer than it is.
The body is a single piece, pale gray, with no face and nothing in front of the
place where the face would be — only a dark band at forehead height, which is
where it looks from, and the band turns on its own when the woman moves. The
arms are thin and too long for the trunk, and the lower hand is lined with
something soft that makes a small rubbery sound when it closes on her forearm.

It stays half a step behind and on the side of the bad hand. When the woman
stops, it stops with her, without anticipating and without lagging — and Rita,
who took twelve years to learn not to pull, looked at that and thought it was
well done, and then for a while could not manage to think anything else.

That is another modality. It has another name on the roster, it comes in by
another door, and the door is an assessment that Teodor would have to ask for.

He does not ask. He waits to be asked.

And the arithmetic, which Rita did once without wanting to have done it: her
shift is three hours, three times a week. The thing in the building across the
street stays all day, and it has no other side of the district waiting for it at
half past eleven.

What she actually does, in order: opens the bedroom window, which he cannot
reach. Strips Thursday's bedding. Helps him in the bath, which is the part the
two of them settled long ago by treating it as service and not as intimacy,
because it is the only way for both of them to come out of it whole. Rubs the
cream into his back — and he said once, in the second year, that her hand is
always the same temperature, and that this is good, because the others used to
arrive cold in winter. Rita said it was the cream. It is not the cream, and she
did not correct it afterward. She checks the three boxes of pills against the
list, a check she does out loud, always, saying the name and the time, and he
answers *right* — and she does it out loud because that is how she learned and
because he likes it.

Then she makes coffee.

The coffee is not on the roster.

---

He tells the same story about twice a month and she lets him.

It is the one about the judge's wardrobe. A wardrobe in imbuia, ordered in 1994,
with a door that would not close — and the problem was not the door, it was the
floor of the judge's house, which sloped almost a centimeter in two meters, and
the judge swore it did not. Teodor took a spirit level, put it on the floor of
the man's living room, and stood there without a word, waiting for him to look.

At that part he always stops. Every time. He leaves the silence the same size.

"And then?" she asks, every time.

"And then he looked."

And then the two of them laugh, and hers comes out louder than she expected,
which happens every time, because she knows exactly what is coming and laughs
like that anyway.

---

That Saturday he had been different since the coffee.

Rita recognizes it the way you recognize something in a person you see three
times a week: he was answering short. He had the cup in his right hand and kept
turning the cup.

She left it alone. You do not pull. If you pull, the person closes up and you
lose the day.

At half past ten she was folding the towel and he said, to the table:

"My dear."

"Sir."

"What day is it today?"

"Saturday. The twelfth."

He nodded, slowly, and stayed like that a little longer.

And then:

"It's seven months since I've been down."

---

Rita stopped with the towel in her hand.

Teodor's building has a shaft, and the shaft is one of the old ones.

It is a real car, metal, with a door that slides to the side and a strip of
light in the frame that blinks when it is about to close. It holds four people
if nobody is carrying anything. It goes up with a low rumble and takes eleven
seconds between the ground floor and the fourth, and halfway up it gives a jolt
that everyone in that building already waits for with their knees.

And it stops at the half-landing.

That was what was authorized in 2038. The second stage — the one that would
have taken the shaft up to the landings — was never authorized, so the car
comes to rest half a flight below Teodor's door and whoever gets out still
climbs six steps. It is not a defect. It is working exactly as it was approved.

In Kalden Rita has ridden the new ones. They have no car: there is an open well
in the middle of the building and a pale-floored platform that rises with no
walls at all, with a strip of light running across the floor in front of a
person's foot. Seven floors in four seconds and without a sound, and when she
stepped off the first one she stood with both hands open at her sides, feeling
for a handrail that is not there.

From the half-landing to the ground floor of Teodor's building there are
nineteen steps and a single handrail, on the right. It is the emergency stair,
which is concrete and has nothing else, because an emergency stair exists in
every building because of the regulations and not because anyone is going to
use it.

Rita knows these numbers because she climbs it three times a week.

With him, with the walker, with one person holding him on one side: forty
minutes to go down and come back up. Maybe fifty.

The shift ended at eleven. The next shift was at half past eleven, on the other
side of Marvik, and she was not going to make it either way, and she would get a
lateness warning, which goes on the record and cannot be explained to anyone.

"Do you want to go down?"

And then he did something she did not expect, which was to get angry.

"I didn't ask for anything," he said. "I said it's been seven months."

"I know."

"I'm not asking, Rita."

"I know, Mr. Teodor."

He struck the table with his right hand, once, without force.

"Because it's none of your business to go down with me. That isn't your job. I
know what your job is, it's written there on the paper the girl left, I read
it." His voice was louder. "I'm not one of those who goes around asking."

---

She sat down in the second chair, the one from nine years later.

"Do you have a coat?"

He took about three seconds.

"I have the gray one."

"Then put on the gray one."

---

It took forty-five minutes.

There was nothing moving about it. It was hard, it was slow, and on the eighth
step he swore in a way she had never heard and apologized straight after, and she
said swearing on stairs does not count. At the half-landing they stopped for
almost two minutes so he could breathe, and he kept staring at the wall because
he did not want to look at her while he was breathing like that.

Then he sat on the bench in front of the building.

And stayed.

He did not talk. He did not look at anything in particular. He sat on a
concrete bench, in the gray coat, on a Saturday morning, with the street going
by.

The bread cart went by. A woman went by with two dogs and one of the dogs
pulled. A boy went by on a bicycle who nearly ran into the curb and caught
himself, and Teodor made a small sound with his mouth, the way a man who has
worked with his hands does when he sees someone almost drop something.

Eighteen minutes.

Then he said *all right*, and they went up.

---

She arrived twenty-three minutes late for the half-past-eleven shift and got
the warning, and the warning went in.

On the way, on the 12, she thought a thing that stayed with her the rest of the
day and that she never told anyone, not Elias, not later.

He had not asked to go anywhere. He did not ask to see the river, did not ask
to visit anyone, did not ask to go to a son's house — he has a son, in the
north, who calls on Sundays. He wanted to sit on the bench in front of his own
building and watch the bread cart go by.

That cost forty-five minutes and one person holding him on one side.

---

At the door, before she left, he already had his left hand under the table
again, and he said:

"Will you be coming Tuesday, ma'am?"

He had never called her ma'am before.

"I will."

"All right, then."

And then, when she already had the key in the lock, from the other room:

"I wrote something. Last week."

Rita waited.

But he said nothing more, and it was not the time to pull, and she was
twenty-three minutes late and had an address on the other side of Marvik.

"Tuesday, Mr. Teodor."

"Tuesday, my dear."
