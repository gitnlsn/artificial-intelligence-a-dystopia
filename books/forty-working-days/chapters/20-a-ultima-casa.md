The shift had moved to seven.

She saw it on Friday night and thought it strange for about ten seconds.
Saturday at seven is too early for a man of seventy-nine who sleeps badly; she
had been arriving at eight for four years. But times change, and times change
all the time, and she had a new house on Tuesday and a week with two shifts
fewer, and she put the strangeness away in the same drawer where she keeps all
the others.

In the downstairs hall there was a printed notice, stuck to the glass of the
board, saying that on Wednesday from eight to noon there would be measurements
for the second stage of the work, and that residents did not need to be home.

It was written above the opening of the emergency stair as well, for those who
read there, and the one up there had more to it — Rita saw the length of the text
and did not stop to read it, because it was two minutes past seven, and she
climbs those nineteen steps in fifty seconds, and that day she climbed them in
fifty seconds.

It is not a calculation she makes. It is the number.

He was already awake. He had a shirt on, which at seven in the morning is not
usual.

"Good morning, Mr. Teodor."

"Good morning, my dear."

The left hand was under the table.

It does not open more than two thirds and it is not going to. That was said to
him at some point, by someone, in a place where such things are said, and he
never told Rita how it was said or who said it. She knows of people who got back
more. Everyone knows of people who got back more. His three were done, on
schedule, and none of them failed. There was no error anywhere. It is one of
those things that are known without anyone knowing from where, and about which
there is nobody to ask, and Rita never asked, because asking is opening
something she has no way of closing before ten.

---

It went the same.

The bedroom window, the sheet, the bath, the cream on his back.

The cream from her hand is three times in a three-hour shift: when she arrives,
after the bath, before the coffee. There has never been a fourth, and there has
never been a shift in which one of the three did not fit. She checked the three
boxes out loud, saying name and time, and he answered *right* to all of them,
and to one of them he answered before she had finished speaking, which he does
when he is in a good mood.

The coffee was at twenty past nine, which is the time for coffee.

He told the one about the judge's wardrobe.

She knew he was going to tell it. He had told it three weeks before, which means
it was outside his schedule, and she noticed that — noticed and made nothing of
it, because old men tell stories and have no calendar — and she listened to the
end, and at the right place she asked:

"And then?"

"And then he looked."

And the two of them laughed.

---

At nine forty he went to the bedroom and took a while.

He took about four minutes, which with the walker is a lot, and she did not go
after him because he hates people coming after him.

He came back with something in his right hand.

---

It was a little box.

The size of half a loaf. Dark wood, four corners, with a sliding lid — and Rita
knows that lid, because there is one just like it, big, on the sewing box that
was his wife's, which stands on the bookcase in the living room.

He put it on the table and pushed it with two fingers to the middle.

"Here."

"What is it?"

"It's yours."

Rita laughed, the way you laugh when you still think it is a joke.

"Mr. Teodor."

"Here," he said again. "There's nothing inside."

She took it. It was light and it was well made, and the lid slid with that
small sound of wood on wood, and slid all the way without catching.

"I made that when I learned to make the lid," he said. "Seventy-three,
seventy-four. You'd make about ten before one came out that slid. That was the
first one that slid."

"You've kept it since seventy-four?"

"I kept it."

---

She tried to give it back.

She tried the right way, without drama, saying it was his, that it was from his
time, that it should stay with his son.

And Teodor said:

"My son doesn't know what that is."

He said it without any hurt at all. He said it the way you give a measurement.

"He thinks it's cute. He thinks everything's cute. He took a stool of mine and
put a plant on it." He shrugged with his right shoulder. "You know what it is."

"I know what a sliding lid is, Mr. Teodor. I don't know anything else."

"You know it's hard." He tapped the table with his finger, once. "That's
enough."

---

She put it in the bag, in the outside pocket, with the paper folded in three.

She did not understand. She stood there for a few minutes with the feeling of
receiving something too big for the reason given, and decided that was just how
it was, that old people give things, that Mrs. Eszter had already tried to give
her a serving dish twice.

His face was normal. She checked that afterward, many times, and always arrived
at the same answer: his face was absolutely normal.

---

At five past ten she washed the two cups and picked up the bag.

"Do you need anything else before I go?"

"No, my dear."

"I left the bedroom window open a finger. Close it before you go to sleep."

"I'll close it."

She went to the door.

And it was there, with her hand on the doorknob, that he said:

"Rita."

She turned. He uses her name about twice a year.

"Thank you," he said.

"What for?"

He took a few seconds.

"For the stairs."

---

She smiled.

"Oh, that." She settled the strap. "We'll do it again. One of these Saturdays."

"Yes."

"I'll come Tuesday."

"Tuesday," he said.

---

The emergency stair has nineteen steps and a single handrail, on the right.

She went down fast, because she was late for nothing, and at the half-landing —
on the landing where they had stopped for almost two minutes that Saturday
morning, where he had kept staring at the wall because he did not want to look
at her while he was breathing like that — she stopped.

She does not know why.

She stood for about five seconds with her hand on the rail, looking at the
flight below, with something at the level of her stomach that she could not
name and that was not hurry and was not tiredness.

Then she went down the rest.

---

On the sidewalk she opened the bag and checked that the little box had stayed in
the right place, and it had.

She slid the lid once, with her thumb, right there, standing in front of the
building, beside the concrete bench where he had sat for eighteen minutes.

It slid all the way, without catching.

She closed the bag and walked on.
