At six forty she was already under the bakery's awning, which is where you
wait, because there is shade and because from there you can see the whole
corner.

The roster opens at six. If anything comes, it comes between six and a quarter
past seven; what comes after that is nothing you can live on. And when it
comes, it comes with ninety seconds to accept. Ninety seconds is plenty of time
if you have the phone in your hand and not much time if you have the phone in
your pocket, and that is why Rita waits with the phone in her hand, the screen
dark, her thumb over it.

She could accept without touching anything. Everybody accepts without touching
anything. Rita uses the phone on purpose, because once, in the first year, she
swore she had accepted a shift and the shift was not on record, and there was
nothing she could show anyone. With the thumb there is a place where she
touched and a time when she touched. She has never needed it. She keeps doing
it.

Of the ninety seconds she has never used the ninety. Not once, in twelve years,
and it is not discipline: it is that the hand is already there.

That morning the screen did not light.

She checked twice whether she had a signal, the way you check, knowing that
you do.

---

Her bag weighs five kilos and always has.

Inside: two pairs of gloves, the apron folded, a small towel, and her own
soap — that she learned in the first year, that there are houses with no soap
and that asking for soap is something that embarrasses both people. A
half-liter bottle. A banana, which she eats at ten, and which that day she did
not know if she would eat at ten.

The banana comes home more often than not. She takes it anyway, because a
caregiver who spends three hours in a house and eats nothing is something
families notice, and noticing leads to talk, and talk leads to being late.

And the hand cream. That is the thing she does not lend and does not leave at
home. Someone who washes her hands eighteen times a day has a choice between
putting on cream and having the skin crack between her fingers, and it is not
vanity, it is a tool: a cracked hand hurts when it holds someone's arm, and the
person being held can tell.

---

At seven o'clock the bakery rolled up its shutters and the young man put the
washer out on the sidewalk.

That is all he does, put it out. It is the size of a carry-on bag, flat, and
after that it goes by itself: it comes out of the door, lines up against the
curb, and goes from the awning to the corner and back, three times, always along
the same path. It wets, scrubs and dries in the same pass, leaves half a meter
of dry sidewalk behind it, and the water that is left runs into the gutter.
Inside it hums; the noise on the outside is the brushes.

It does not go into the corners. The young man does the corners afterwards,
crouching, with a cloth.

Rita took three steps to the side without thinking, like someone who has done
it many times.

The 12 went by at six past seven with people standing.

The 12 is a line and stops at stops, and that is why she takes the 12. Anything
that is not a line does not come down in Kalden at that hour for a price that
fits inside a shift, and Rita knows the price by heart the way you know the
price of something you do not buy.

At nine past seven three went over, in single file, at the height of the sixth
floor.

They are the size of a small car and have no wings and no propellers: they have
four round housings at the corners, tilted forward when they are going fast,
and underneath they are smooth and pale. They make a sound of air being pushed,
and the sound arrives after them. The shadows of the three crossed the awning
one after another, very fast, and the young man did not even lift his head from
the corner he was cleaning.

They go to the other side of the river. They come down in upper Kalden and in
the center, on platforms, and they do not come down in Brenna, and Rita knows
that the way you know where the bus does not go.

She did not look up. Nobody looks up.

There was another woman waiting on the corner below, her back turned, older,
with a bag like hers. They do not talk to each other. It is not dislike. It is
that whoever is waiting is allocated within the same radius, and whoever is
waiting next to you is whoever can get what did not come to you, and both of
them know it, and the subject is a bad one, and so one waits at one end of the
block and the other at the other.

At twenty past seven the woman left.

Rita stayed.

---

It used to be three, four a day. She knows the exact week it started to drop
because she wrote it down — wrote it in a little notebook, by hand, at the
beginning, because a thing written by hand is the only record of hers that is
not also somewhere else, and then she stopped writing it down because writing
it down made the thing too visible.

Nobody said anything to her. She has tried to explain this to maybe three
people and has never managed it: **nobody said anything.** There was no
letter, there was no warning, she was not called in anywhere, there is not one
complaint from any family against her. She checked. She is good at what she
does, and she knows she is, and the people she has looked after like her and
some of them call afterwards.

It simply comes less.

And then you start doing what she does, which is building theories on your own
under an awning at half past seven in the morning. That maybe it is because she
turned down two shifts in July, and she turned them down because that week her
left hand would not close properly and you cannot hold anyone's arm with a hand
that does not close. That maybe it is the address. That maybe it is because she
takes longer, and she does take longer, she takes longer because she talks, and
talking does not count anywhere.

Then she stops building theories, because there is nowhere to hand a theory in.

---

At seven fifty-two she was still there.

She was not waiting anymore, and that is the part she was slow to admit. At
seven fifty-two nothing else comes. She was standing under an awning with a
five-kilo bag on her shoulder because she had nowhere to go, which is different
from waiting, and the two things look so alike from outside that it is possible
to do one thinking you are doing the other.

That was when they opened the window.

Fourth floor, the building on the corner, the brick one with the crooked
air-conditioning boxes. Someone threw it wide open — Friday morning, the smell
of cleaning product coming down with it — and put music on loud the way you put
music on loud when you are going to clean the whole apartment.

And the music came down.

---

The first thing she did was check whether it was for her.

She did not think it in those words; her body did it on its own, the way a hand
goes to a pocket. She looked around for the place it had been sent from and
there was no place at all, and it took her a good five seconds to understand
that she was looking for the wrong thing.

It was coming from the window. That was all. Someone had opened a window.

---

Rita knew it.

Not the name of the song. That she never knew and does not know to this day.
She knows it is from the record her mother played in the car, the one that
lived in the glove compartment, and she knows her mother said Cardigans with
the accent of someone making the accent up, and she knows her mother liked this
one more than the others and skipped ahead until she got to it.

The car she does not know. She has tried, without effort, the way you try to
remember something unimportant while you are waiting for something else: the
color, the seat, whether it was morning. It does not come. The glove
compartment comes, the accent comes, the order of the songs comes. The rest was
never there, and not once has she found that strange.

A woman singing slowly, without forcing anything. A guitar that does the same
thing about four times before it changes. A big, soft bass underneath. And that
thing about music from another country that Rita has never been able to say what
it is — a sadness that is not apologizing for being sadness.

From where she was, the bass was the loudest thing. It moved more than the
woman singing, and the drums always came in a little late.

She stood still.

---

It was not beautiful and she did not cry. There was nothing cinematic about it:
she stood on a wet sidewalk with a bag on her shoulder listening to a song
somebody had put on to clean the apartment.

What happened was duller and much worse.

She realized that it had reached her **without being sent.**

Nobody chose for Rita to hear it. The person on the fourth floor did not know she
existed, did not know there was someone under the awning, was not offering
anything to anyone. They opened the window because they were going to clean the
apartment. The music fell into the street because music falls, and it fell on
her by accident, the same way it would have fallen on anyone.

Rita tried to remember the last time something had reached her like that, and
could not.

Everything that reaches her is for her. The shift is for her. The notice is for
her. What plays when she puts in her earbuds is for her, chosen, adjusted, and
always good, and always exactly good, and she had never noticed that *exactly
good* is something you receive and not something you find.

That, there, was not for her. It was just loud.

---

She stayed until it ended. It took about three and a half minutes.

Then another one started, one she did not know, and the woman on the fourth
floor turned it down a little, and the spell came apart in the silly way these
things come apart.

Rita settled the strap on her shoulder.

And it was then, and only then, that she noticed.

She had been standing still for an hour and twenty minutes.

Not *waiting* — still. Not walking, not carrying anyone, not on the way to
anywhere, her body in one place. She kept trying to work out when the last time
had been and the sum would not come out, because at home she sits but at home
she sits *doing something*, and on the bus she rides sitting but going, and on
a Sunday with no shift she stays home, and staying home is not this.

Months. Maybe since the start of the year.

And she held that in her hand for a few seconds and could not call it rest,
because it was not. She had not stopped. **She had been stopped.** The first
hour and twenty she had spent standing in one place in who knows how many
months was not her choice, it was what was left of a morning when the screen
did not light.

Rita put the phone in her pocket, which she does not do in the mornings.

Then she walked down the street toward the 12, which would take her home at half
past eight on a Friday, with the five-kilo bag, with the soap inside, without
having worked.
