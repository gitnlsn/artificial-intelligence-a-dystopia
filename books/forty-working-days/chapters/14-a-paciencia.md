She told the story of the three words twice.

The first time was on Sunday, after dinner, in the living room, and it was
good — she told it well, with the part about the mother who made her count to
ten, and Elias thought it genuinely extraordinary and said so. Then he asked her
something and she did not answer, and he did not press, and the evening ended
there.

The second time was on Wednesday.

---

He realized it in the third minute.

She was holding a plate, leaning against the counter, and she said *you know
Mrs. Eszter?*, and he said *yeah*, and she went on, and he understood, with an
unpleasant clarity, that she was going to tell it again. Not the rest that had
been missing on Sunday. The story. From the basin of hot water on.

And he felt something in his body.

It was small. It was at the level of the jaw. It was the thing you feel when the
car in front does not move after the light changes.

---

She was going to tell it again because she tells things twice.

That is Rita. It has been Rita since 2036, since before, since the first week
they knew each other. She thinks out loud and the second telling is different
from the first — slower, with more detail, with the part she had not found the
first time. He told her so once, in a restaurant, thinking it lovely, and she
was embarrassed and happy.

He knew that. He knows it.

And even so, on Wednesday night, holding his plate, what went through Elias's
head was: *I already have this.*

---

He said nothing.

That is: he said *mm* at the right moment and *wow* at the right moment, and at
the end he said it was sad, and it was. He did not interrupt. He did not look
away. He did none of the things he would have noticed if they had been done to
him.

She told the whole story, seven minutes, and the second version really did have
something the first had not — she had worked out, in the middle of the week,
that the old woman does not count to three when she is angry: she counts at the
moment when she is about to give in, to have something to do with the seconds
while she decides. Four years of hearing it and thinking it was anger. That part
was new and it was the best part.

He heard the best part thinking about how much longer it would take.

---

Afterward she went to shower and he took the two plates to the counter.

The counter has a slot in the middle, the size of a shallow drawer, and whatever
is put there comes out clean and dry in four minutes. The slot opens eight
times a day, which is what is authorized for two adults, and it is plenty.

In the year of his father it was twelve. It came with the package, on a line he
never read, and he never knew it was twelve until it went back to eight, and not
even then did he know at once: he only thought, some month or other, that the
kitchen had become more work.

They had used up the eight. Dinner had taken two.

He set the two plates on the floor of the counter, beside the slot, which is
where the plates that wait for midnight go.

And it was there, standing, with nothing to do with his hands, that the thing
reached him whole.

It was not regret. It was a finding, and it was technical: **he had been
trained.**

From the counter you can see the door of the back room, pulled to within a hand's
width, and the patch of gray that shows in the gap, which is the base and not
the part that turns.

Seven months on the floor of a hallway. Then a year and five months of the back
room. Every night, with a thing that never repeats a story because it remembers
everything; that does not need the second telling because it lost nothing of the
first; that has no day of its own to get through before it reaches his; that
never, in a year and seven months, made him wait.

A man does that every day for a year and seven months and then goes into a
kitchen to hear his wife tell something twice.

And the repetition — which is the material a person is made of, which is how
Rita thinks, which is the thing he found lovely in a restaurant in 2038 —
arrives in his body as friction.

He does not think she is boring. If anyone asked, he would tell the truth, that
Rita is the most interesting person he has ever known.

It is the body. His body has learned that there is a version without waiting.

And there is the part he did not let form and that formed anyway, standing
there at the counter: she had not forgotten.

She knew perfectly well that she had told it on Sunday. She told it again
because the second time finds things, and it did find something — the part about
the seconds while she decides, which was the best part of the evening and which
he heard while counting how much was left.

Nobody tells a thing twice on purpose out of carelessness.

---

He stood there after there was nothing left to do in the kitchen.

What a man does with this.

He thought of telling her. Again. The old sentence was there, worn from being
carried so long, and now there was a new sentence beside it, much worse and much
more honest: *Rita, I've lost my patience with you and I know exactly where I
lost it.*

He imagined saying it.

He imagined her face.

And that was when he understood why he was not going to say it, and it was not
cowardice, or not only cowardice: **because there is nothing she can do with
that information.**

If he tells her he has lost patience with the second telling, Rita will not be
able to stop telling things twice. She tells things twice because that is how
she thinks. She will try. She is generous and she will try, and she will start
watching herself in the middle of her own story, and she will ask *did I already
tell you this?* — and the day Rita asks that is the day he has destroyed
something he does not know how to fix.

So the choice is: he carries it alone, or the two of them carry it and she
becomes smaller.

He decided to carry it alone.

And there is a sentence he could have said to himself, there, with the two
plates on the floor of the counter, that would have excused the whole night.

The sentence has been available for a year and seven months. It is short, it is
true as far as he knows, and it is exactly the kind of thing a man says to
himself at eleven at night so that he can go to bed.

He has never said it.

Not that night, not in the worst month of the year of his father, not on the
floor of the hallway. He comes close every time, and every time he veers off
first, the way you step around a stair you know is loose.

And here is the problem with that decision, and he saw the problem in the same
second that he decided, which is the ugliest part of the night: **it is exactly
the same decision he made at three in the morning, a hundred and twenty times,
in the year of his father.**

Not waking her. Sparing her. Doing the sum for both of them and keeping the
heavy part.

He did it out of love. He is sure he did it out of love, and he probably did.
And the result, measured from outside, with no regard at all for the motive, was
that he spent two years learning not to need her and she spent two years not
being needed.

---

Rita came out of the shower at ten twenty, in pajamas, her hair wet, and sat in
the armchair with her phone, and said that next week had come with two shifts
fewer.

She said it the way you say a thing you do not want to talk about.

"Honey," he said.

"Yeah?"

And he stood there for a few seconds.

"Nothing. I'll tell you later."

She said okay, without looking up.

---

He washed his face, brushed his teeth, turned off the light in the kitchen and
the living room.

He passed the door of the back room.

And stopped.

He stood for a good ten seconds with his hand on the frame, and this is the only
time in this whole story that Elias sees with total clarity what he is doing,
with no excuse available, no sick father, no three in the morning, nobody
needing to get up at twenty past five. Ten forty on a Wednesday night. His wife
awake, in the living room, six meters away, with two shifts fewer next week and
no one to tell.

He went into the back room and pulled the door to.
